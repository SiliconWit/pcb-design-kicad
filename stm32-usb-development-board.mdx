A board you solder yourself is limited by your hands. A chip with 48 pins, each half a millimetre from the next, is beyond most soldering irons. A factory can make and assemble such a board for the price of a meal, if you send it the right files. In this lesson you design a development board around the STM32F103C8T6 in the well-known Blue Pill shape, with USB, a debug connector and two crystals. Then you prepare the three kinds of file a factory needs and order assembled boards. #KiCad #STM32 #PCBDesign

A fast chip takes its current in sharp pulses, so each of its supply pins gets its own small capacitor, placed right beside the pin:

```text
  +3V3 ──┬───────────┬───────────┬──────
         │           │           │
       pin 24      pin 36      pin 48     STM32 VDD pins
         │           │           │
     C5 ─┴─      C6 ─┴─      C7 ─┴─       100nF each,
        ─┬─         ─┬─         ─┬─       within 2 mm
         │           │           │        of its pin
  GND ───┴───────────┴───────────┴──────
```

## STM32 Fundamentals

<InArticleAd />


The STM32F103C8T6 is a 32-bit ARM Cortex-M3 microcontroller from STMicroelectronics. It has 64 KB of program memory (flash) and 20 KB of working memory (SRAM), with USB, I2C, SPI, UART, an analog converter and timers built in. In the part number, C means 48 pins and 8 means 64 KB of flash. T6 means the flat LQFP package and the industrial temperature range.

### Clock Tree

The chip can take its clock from several sources, and the board must provide the ones the design uses:

- **HSI** (High Speed Internal): an 8 MHz oscillator inside the chip. It needs no parts but is not accurate enough for USB.
- **HSE** (High Speed External): a crystal of 4 to 16 MHz on the board. This board uses 8 MHz.
- **PLL** (phase-locked loop): a circuit in the chip that multiplies a clock. It multiplies the 8 MHz of the HSE by 9 to give the 72 MHz system clock.
- **LSE** (Low Speed External): a 32.768 kHz crystal for the real-time clock (RTC). Divided by two 15 times, this frequency gives exactly one pulse a second.

### Why STM32 Matters for PCB Designers

Many commercial products use an STM32, and this board has the problems of most modern boards: fine pins, decoupling at every supply pin, two crystals and USB.

### Power Supply: USB 5V to 3.3V

The STM32F103 works only up to 3.6 V on its supply. USB gives 5 V, so a regulator must make 3.3 V from it. The AMS1117-3.3 is a linear regulator of the low-dropout kind (LDO): it has three pins, needs only two capacitors, and loses the difference of 1.7 V as heat.

<Steps>
1. **Place the USB Mini-B connector.** Add `USB_B_Mini` from the Connector library. Its pins are VBUS (5V), D+ and D- (the two data lines) and GND.

2. **Place the AMS1117-3.3 regulator** (SOT-223 package). Connect VBUS to its input and a `+3V3` power symbol to its output.

3. **Add its two capacitors.** Place C11 (10 µF) from the regulator input to `GND` and C12 (10 µF) from the regulator output to `GND`. This regulator can oscillate without a capacitor at its output.

4. **Add power symbols.** Place `+5V` on the USB VBUS net, `+3V3` on the regulator output, and `GND` on every ground connection.

5. **Add the power LED.** Connect D1 and R4 in series from +3V3 to GND, so the LED lights when 3.3V is present.
</Steps>

### MCU and Decoupling

A chip that switches 72 million times a second takes its current in pulses a few nanoseconds long. The track from the regulator has inductance, which resists a fast change of current, so for a moment the voltage at the pin dips and the chip can reset or make errors. A 100 nF decoupling capacitor at the pin is a local store of charge that supplies the pulse. It works only if it is close: as a rough guide, each millimetre of track adds about 1 nH, and a 50 mA pulse that rises in 5 ns loses 10 mV across each nanohenry.

The chip has three VDD pins, an analog supply pin (VDDA) and a backup supply pin (VBAT). Each gets its own capacitor.

<Steps>
1. **Place the STM32F103C8T6.** Add `STM32F103C8Tx` from the MCU_ST_STM32F1 library.

2. **Connect the supply pins.** Wire VDD (pins 24, 36, 48) and VDDA (pin 9) to `+3V3`. Wire VSS, the ground pins (23, 35, 47), and VSSA (pin 8) to `GND`.

3. **Add the decoupling capacitors.** Place 100 nF capacitors C5, C6 and C7 at VDD pins 24, 36 and 48, C8 at VDDA (pin 9) and C9 at VBAT (pin 1). Add C14 (1 µF) at VDDA as well, because the analog converter needs an especially quiet supply. Add C10 (4.7 µF) near the MCU as a larger store that refills the small capacitors.

4. **VBAT pin.** Connect VBAT (pin 1) to `+3V3`. This pin keeps the real-time clock running. A later design can connect a coin cell here.
</Steps>

### HSE Crystal (8 MHz)

A crystal keeps its marked frequency only when it sees the load capacitance its maker specifies. Two capacitors and the board's stray capacitance make that load.

<Steps>
1. **Place the 8 MHz crystal** between OSC_IN (pin 5) and OSC_OUT (pin 6).

2. **Add load capacitors.** Place C1 (20 pF) from OSC_IN to GND and C2 (20 pF) from OSC_OUT to GND. The two capacitors act in series, so each is twice the load that remains after the stray capacitance of pins and tracks is taken away: $C = 2\,(C_L - C_{\text{stray}})$, where $C_L$ is the specified load capacitance and $C_{\text{stray}}$ is about 5 pF. A crystal with 20 pF load capacitance needs $2 \times (20 - 5) = 30\,\text{pF}$ capacitors. The 20 pF capacitors on this board suit a crystal with 15 pF load capacitance.

3. **No series resistor.** For most crystals the STM32F103 needs no resistor between its pins and the crystal.
</Steps>

### LSE Crystal (32.768 kHz)

<Steps>
1. **Place the 32.768 kHz crystal** between OSC32_IN (pin 3) and OSC32_OUT (pin 4).

2. **Add load capacitors.** Place C3 (6.8 pF) from OSC32_IN to GND and C4 (6.8 pF) from OSC32_OUT to GND. This oscillator runs on very little power and stops easily, so use the values the crystal's data sheet gives.
</Steps>

:::tip[Crystal placement]
In the layout, place both crystals as close to the MCU as you can. A long track adds stray capacitance and can stop the oscillator, the 32.768 kHz one most easily.
:::

### Reset Circuit

NRST restarts the chip when it is pulled to 0 V. The resistor holds it high, and the capacitor absorbs noise spikes that would restart the chip.

<Steps>
1. **NRST pin** (pin 7). Connect a 100 nF capacitor (C13) from NRST to GND.

2. **Pull-up resistor.** Connect R2 (10 kΩ) from NRST to +3V3.

3. **Reset button.** Place an SMD tactile switch (SW1) from NRST to GND.
</Steps>

### USB Circuit

USB carries data on two wires, D+ and D-. The STM32F103 has a full-speed (12 Mbps) USB circuit inside, on pins PA11 (D-) and PA12 (D+). To know that a device is present, the computer watches the two wires for a steady voltage, and one resistor on the board provides it.

<Steps>
1. **Wire the USB data lines.** Connect the USB connector D- to PA11 and D+ to PA12.

2. **D+ pull-up resistor.** Place R1 (1.5 kΩ) from PA12 (D+) to +3V3. A pull-up on D+ means "a full-speed device is here". The value 1.5 kΩ is fixed by the USB standard.

3. **Protection against static discharge is optional** on a development board. To add it, place a USBLC6-2SC6 on the data lines.
</Steps>

:::caution[USB D+ pull-up is mandatory]
Without this resistor the board powers on and the computer never shows a USB device. Some STM32 chips have the pull-up inside. The STM32F103 does not.
:::

### SWD Debug Header

SWD (serial wire debug) is how a program enters this chip. Through two signal pins, a debugger such as an ST-Link writes the flash and can stop and inspect the program.

<Steps>
1. **Place a 1x4 pin header** (J2) with the pins SWDIO, SWCLK, GND and 3V3.

2. **Wire SWDIO** to PA13 (pin 34) and **SWCLK** to PA14 (pin 37). These pins are SWD pins after every reset.

3. **Connect GND and 3V3** on the header. Through the 3V3 pin the debugger measures the board's voltage, and it can also power the board.
</Steps>

### Boot Configuration

At each reset the chip reads two pins, Boot0 and Boot1, to decide where to start: your program in flash, or a built-in loader that accepts a new program over a serial port.

| Boot1 | Boot0 | Mode |
|-------|-------|------|
| X | 0 | Boot from flash (normal operation) |
| 0 | 1 | Boot from system memory (UART bootloader on USART1) |
| 1 | 1 | Boot from SRAM |

<Steps>
1. **Boot0 jumper.** Place a 1x3 pin header (JP1) with its center pin on Boot0 (pin 44), one outer pin on GND (normal start) and the other on +3V3 (built-in loader). A jumper cap selects one. Add R3 (100 kΩ) from Boot0 to GND, so the board starts from flash when no jumper is fitted.

2. **Boot1.** Connect Boot1 (pin 20, PB2) to GND. Starting from SRAM is rarely needed.
</Steps>

### User LED and GPIO Headers

<Steps>
1. **User LED.** Connect D2 and R5 (330 Ω) in series from +3V3 to PC13 (pin 2). The LED lights when the program sets PC13 low, because current then flows from +3V3 through the LED into the pin.

2. **GPIO headers.** Place two 1x20 pin headers (J3 and J4). Connect all remaining GPIO pins to them in the order of the Blue Pill, and include +3V3, +5V and GND pins.

3. **Run the electrical rules check (ERC)** and correct every error and warning.
</Steps>

## PCB Layout

<InArticleAd />


### Board Setup

<Steps>
1. **Board outline.** On the Edge.Cuts layer, draw a 55 x 25 mm rectangle. This is the Blue Pill shape: narrow enough to sit across the middle of a breadboard.

2. **Layer setup.** The board has two copper layers, F.Cu (front) and B.Cu (back). Check in **Board Setup > Board Stackup > Physical Stackup** that it shows two copper layers on 1.6mm FR-4, the usual glass and epoxy board material.

3. **Design rules for JLCPCB.** A design rule is a limit KiCad holds you to. Here the limits come from what the factory can make. Set them in **Board Setup > Design Rules**:
   - Minimum track width: 0.15 mm
   - Minimum clearance: 0.15 mm
   - Minimum via drill: 0.3 mm
   - Minimum via diameter: 0.6 mm
   - Default track width: 0.25 mm for signals, a little narrower than the 0.3 mm pads of the chip, and 0.4 mm for supply tracks, which carry more current
</Steps>

:::tip[JLCPCB design rules]
JLCPCB's standard process makes tracks and gaps down to 0.127 mm and via holes down to 0.3 mm. The rules above stay just above these limits.
:::

### Component Placement

<Steps>
1. **Update PCB from schematic** (F8). All footprints appear in one group.

2. **USB connector** at the middle of one short edge, with its opening toward the outside.

3. **Regulator (U2)** close to the USB connector, with C11 and C12 against its input and output pins. Its large tab carries its heat into the copper, so give the tab a wide copper area.

4. **MCU (U1)** in the center. Turn it so that PA11 and PA12 face the USB connector, which keeps the USB tracks short.

5. **8 MHz crystal (Y1)** within 5 mm of the OSC_IN and OSC_OUT pins, with C1 and C2 beside it.

6. **32.768 kHz crystal (Y2)** within 3 mm of the OSC32 pins, with C3 and C4 beside it.

7. **Decoupling capacitors** (C5-C9), each within 2 mm of its supply pin. Farther away they stop doing their work.

8. **GPIO headers** (J3, J4) along the two long edges, at 2.54mm pin spacing.

9. **SWD header** (J2) at the short edge opposite the USB connector.

10. **Boot0 jumper** (JP1) and **reset button** (SW1) on the front, where a finger can reach them.
</Steps>

### Routing

<Steps>
1. **USB tracks first.** Route D+ and D- from the connector to PA12 and PA11. The two signals work as a pair, so they must arrive together and see the same surroundings: keep them side by side on one layer, under 25 mm long, equal in length to within 2 mm, 0.3 mm wide, and with no vias.

2. **Crystal tracks.** Make the tracks of both crystals as short as possible, and route no other signal between a crystal and the MCU.

3. **Supply tracks.** Route +3V3 and +5V with 0.4 mm tracks. Keep the path from USB through the regulator short and direct.

4. **Decoupling capacitors.** Route each 100 nF capacitor straight to its VDD pin and to the nearest GND via. The loop from pin to capacitor to ground and back must be as small as you can make it, because the loop is the inductance that slows the current.

5. **GPIO to headers.** Route the remaining pins to the headers with 0.25 mm tracks. These signals are slow, so any reasonable path works.

6. **Ground pour.** Add a GND copper zone on F.Cu and on B.Cu and fill them with `B` (Fill All Zones). A large unbroken area of ground copper under the signals is called a ground plane. Every signal's current returns through it by the shortest path, which keeps noise low.

7. **Add stitching vias.** Place GND vias around the board edge, and more near the USB connector and the MCU. They join the front and back pours so the two act as one.

8. **Run the design rules check (DRC).** Correct it until there are no errors and no unconnected nets.
</Steps>

## Manufacturing: First Fab House Order

<InArticleAd />


The factory needs three kinds of file. Gerber and drill files describe the bare board. The bill of materials (BOM) says which part goes at each reference. The placement file says where each part sits and which way it is turned.

### Generating Gerber Files

A Gerber file is a flat picture of one layer: where the copper, the solder mask (the protective coating) or the printed text goes. Every board factory reads this format.

<Steps>
1. **Open the Plot dialog.** In the PCB Editor, go to **File > Plot**.

2. **Select layers to plot:**
   - F.Cu (front copper)
   - B.Cu (back copper)
   - F.Paste (front solder paste, for stencils)
   - B.Paste (back solder paste)
   - F.SilkS (front silkscreen)
   - B.SilkS (back silkscreen)
   - F.Mask (front solder mask)
   - B.Mask (back solder mask)
   - Edge.Cuts (board outline)

3. **Plot settings:**
   - Format: **Gerber**
   - Output directory: `gerbers/`
   - Check "Use Protel filename extensions" (JLCPCB expects this)
   - Check "Subtract soldermask from silkscreen"
   - Coordinate format: 4.6 (unit mm)

4. **Click Plot.** KiCad generates one Gerber file per layer.

5. **Generate drill files.** Click the **Generate Drill Files** button in the Plot dialog.
   - Drill file format: **Excellon**
   - Drill units: **Millimeters**
   - Zeros format: **Decimal format**
   - Check "PTH and NPTH in single file"
   - Click **Generate Drill File**.

6. **Look at the files in a Gerber viewer.** Open the **Gerber Viewer** from the KiCad project manager (or use gerbv). Load all the Gerber files and the drill file. Check that the outline is right, no pad is missing, and each hole sits in the center of its pad. The factory makes exactly what these files show, not what your KiCad project shows.
</Steps>

The set of files, where `project` is your project name:

| File | Layer |
|------|-------|
| `project-F_Cu.gtl`, `project-B_Cu.gbl` | Front and back copper |
| `project-F_Mask.gts`, `project-B_Mask.gbs` | Front and back solder mask |
| `project-F_Silkscreen.gto`, `project-B_Silkscreen.gbo` | Front and back printed text |
| `project-Edge_Cuts.gm1` | Board outline |
| `project.drl` | Holes |

### Generating BOM for SMT Assembly

The factory solders the surface-mount parts (SMT assembly) from the stock of its partner LCSC. So the BOM must give an LCSC part number for every part, in a CSV file (text with commas between columns).

<Steps>
1. **Add the part numbers.** In the Schematic Editor, go to **Tools > Edit Symbol Fields**. Give every part that JLCPCB will solder an LCSC part number in a field named "LCSC" (add the field if it does not exist).

2. **Common LCSC part numbers** for this design:

   | Component | LCSC Part Number |
   |-----------|-----------------|
   | STM32F103C8T6 | C8734 |
   | AMS1117-3.3 | C6186 |
   | 100 nF 0805 | C49678 |
   | 10 µF 0805 | C15850 |
   | 4.7 µF 0805 | C1779 |
   | 1 µF 0805 | C28323 |
   | 20 pF 0805 | C1790 |
   | 6.8 pF 0805 | C1804 |
   | 1.5 kΩ 0805 | C4310 |
   | 10 kΩ 0805 | C17414 |
   | 100 kΩ 0805 | C149504 |
   | 330 Ω 0805 | C23138 |
   | 8 MHz crystal | C16172 |
   | 32.768 kHz crystal | C32346 |

3. **Export the BOM.** Go to **Tools > Generate Bill of Materials** or use a BOM export plugin. The JLCPCB format needs these columns: `Comment`, `Designator`, `Footprint`, `LCSC Part Number`. You can format this manually in a spreadsheet or use a KiCad BOM plugin configured for JLCPCB.

4. **Save as CSV.** Example rows:

   ```csv
   Comment,Designator,Footprint,LCSC
   100nF,"C5,C6,C7,C8,C9,C13",C_0805_2012Metric,C49678
   STM32F103C8T6,U1,LQFP-48_7x7mm_P0.5mm,C8734
   AMS1117-3.3,U2,SOT-223-3_TabPin2,C6186
   ```
</Steps>

### Generating Pick-and-Place (CPL) File

The pick-and-place file (also called the centroid or CPL file) tells the assembly machine, for each part, the position of its center, the face of the board and the angle of rotation. A wrong angle puts an LED or a chip on its pads the wrong way round, with no error message.

<Steps>
1. **Export from KiCad.** In the PCB Editor, go to **File > Fabrication Outputs > Component Placement (.pos file)**.

2. **Settings:**
   - Format: **CSV**
   - Units: **Millimeters**
   - Files: **Single file for board**

3. **Rename the columns for JLCPCB.** KiCad writes `Ref`, `PosX`, `PosY`, `Rot`, `Side`. JLCPCB expects `Designator`, `Mid X`, `Mid Y`, `Rotation`, `Layer`. Change the names in a text editor or a spreadsheet.

4. **Check the rotations after you upload.** KiCad and JLCPCB do not always agree on which direction is 0 degrees for a part. The preview on the order page shows each part as it will be placed.
</Steps>

### JLCPCB Order Walkthrough

<Steps>
1. **Create an account** at [jlcpcb.com](https://jlcpcb.com).

2. **Upload Gerbers.** Click "Order Now" on the homepage. Drag and drop your `gerbers/` folder (or a ZIP of the Gerber and drill files) onto the upload area. JLCPCB's viewer will show a preview of your board.

3. **Set board parameters:**
   - Layers: 2
   - Dimensions: 55 x 25 mm (auto-detected)
   - Quantity: 5 (the minimum; costs roughly 2 to 5 USD)
   - PCB thickness: 1.6 mm
   - Surface finish: HASL (with lead) for cheapest, or LeadFree HASL
   - Solder mask color: green (cheapest and fastest)
   - Silkscreen: white
   - Copper weight: 1 oz
   - Remove order number: "Specify a location" (add a JLCJLCJLCJLC text on your silkscreen where you want it)

4. **Enable SMT assembly.** Toggle "SMT Assembly" to ON.
   - Assembly side: Top
   - Tooling holes: "Added by JLCPCB"
   - Confirm: check the assembly terms

5. **Upload BOM and CPL files.** Click "Add BOM File" and "Add CPL File". Upload the CSV files you generated.

6. **Review component matching.** JLCPCB shows each BOM line matched to a part from their inventory. Green check marks mean the part is in stock. Yellow warnings mean you need to confirm or substitute. Common issues:
   - Extended parts (not in their basic library) cost an extra 3 USD per unique part
   - Out-of-stock parts need substitution with a compatible alternative
   - Confirm the footprint matches for each component

7. **Review placement preview.** JLCPCB shows a 2D rendering with components placed on the board. Check that every component is correctly positioned and rotated. Fix any rotation issues by adjusting the rotation value in your CPL file and re-uploading.

8. **Select shipping** and place the order. DHL and FedEx typically take 5-7 days. Standard shipping is cheaper but takes 2-3 weeks.
</Steps>

## Soldering Remaining Components

<InArticleAd />


The factory solders the surface-mount parts. Its standard service does not fit through-hole parts, so you solder the pin headers yourself.

<Steps>
1. **GPIO headers (J3, J4).** Insert the 1x20 pin headers from the back of the board, so the long pins point down into a breadboard, and solder on the front. Solder one pin at each end first, check that the header stands straight, then solder the rest.

2. **SWD header (J2).** Insert the 1x4 pin header and solder it.

3. **Boot0 jumper (JP1).** Insert the 1x3 pin header and solder it. Fit a jumper cap from the center pin to the GND side, for a normal start from flash.

4. **Inspect your joints** for bridges, dull joints and pins you missed.
</Steps>

## First Power-On and Testing

<InArticleAd />


A short on this board can damage it or the USB port that feeds it. Check with a meter before you plug it in, and use a USB charger or power bank for the first test, not a computer.

<Steps>
1. **Look.** With a magnifier, look for solder that bridges two pins of the MCU or the USB connector. Check that every part is present and the right way round.

2. **Measure with no power connected.** With a multimeter on continuity, check that no two of +5V, +3V3 and GND are joined.

3. **Power the board through USB.** The power LED (D1) lights. If a part becomes hot, unplug at once.

4. **Measure the voltages.** +5V on the VBUS net must be 4.8-5.2V. +3V3 at the regulator output, at the MCU VDD pins and at VDDA must be 3.25-3.35V.

5. **Test the SWD connection.** Connect an ST-Link V2 debugger to the SWD header, and run `st-info --probe` (from the stlink tools) or use STM32CubeProgrammer.

   ```bash
   st-info --probe
   ```

   The output names an STM32F103 device with 64 KB of flash.

6. **Test the reset button.** Press it while the debugger is connected or an LED program runs. The MCU restarts.
</Steps>

## Firmware: USB CDC Serial

<InArticleAd />


This program makes the STM32 appear on the computer as a serial port (the USB class called CDC) and sends a line of text. It tests the crystal, the PLL, the USB tracks and the pull-up resistor together. Use a USB cable that has data wires.

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="STM32CubeIDE">

The quickest way is STM32CubeIDE with its HAL library:

<Steps>
1. **Create a new project** in STM32CubeIDE. Select STM32F103C8Tx as the target.

2. **Configure clocks.** In the .ioc file, set HSE as the clock source, PLL multiplier to x9 (72 MHz), and enable the USB clock at 48 MHz (use the /1.5 prescaler from 72 MHz).

3. **Enable USB.** Under Connectivity, enable USB. Under Middleware, enable USB_DEVICE with Class "Communication Device Class (CDC)".

4. **Generate code.** Click "Generate Code".

5. **Add your message.** Open `USB_DEVICE/App/usbd_cdc_if.c`. In the `CDC_Receive_FS` function (or in a periodic function in `main.c`), add:
</Steps>

```c
/* In main.c, inside the while(1) loop */
extern uint8_t CDC_Transmit_FS(uint8_t* Buf, uint16_t Len);

char msg[] = "Hello from STM32\r\n";
CDC_Transmit_FS((uint8_t*)msg, strlen(msg));
HAL_Delay(1000);
```

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Bare Metal (arm-none-eabi-gcc)">

Without HAL, the libopencm3 library gives a small USB interface. The program below is an outline: the full descriptors are in the library's examples.

<Steps>
1. **Install libopencm3.** Clone the repository and build for STM32F1:

   ```bash
   git clone https://github.com/libopencm3/libopencm3.git
   cd libopencm3
   make TARGETS=stm32/f1
   ```

2. **Create main.c:**
</Steps>

```c
/*
 * main.c - USB CDC "Hello" using libopencm3
 * STM32F103C8T6 @ 72 MHz (8 MHz HSE, PLL x9)
 */

#include <libopencm3/stm32/rcc.h>
#include <libopencm3/stm32/gpio.h>
#include <libopencm3/usb/usbd.h>
#include <libopencm3/usb/cdc.h>
#include <string.h>

static usbd_device *usbd_dev;
static uint8_t serial_buf[128];

/* USB descriptors (CDC ACM) */
static const struct usb_device_descriptor dev_descr = {
    .bLength = USB_DT_DEVICE_SIZE,
    .bDescriptorType = USB_DT_DEVICE,
    .bcdUSB = 0x0200,
    .bDeviceClass = USB_CLASS_CDC,
    .bDeviceSubClass = 0,
    .bDeviceProtocol = 0,
    .bMaxPacketSize0 = 64,
    .idVendor = 0x0483,   /* STMicroelectronics */
    .idProduct = 0x5740,  /* Virtual COM Port */
    .bcdDevice = 0x0100,
    .iManufacturer = 1,
    .iProduct = 2,
    .iSerialNumber = 3,
    .bNumConfigurations = 1,
};

/* (Full CDC descriptors omitted for brevity;
 * see libopencm3 examples/stm32/f1/stm32-h103/usb_cdcacm
 * for the complete descriptor set) */

static void cdcacm_data_rx_cb(usbd_device *dev, uint8_t ep) {
    (void)ep;
    char buf[64];
    int len = usbd_ep_read_packet(dev, 0x01, buf, 64);
    /* Echo received data back */
    if (len > 0) {
        usbd_ep_write_packet(dev, 0x82, buf, len);
    }
}

static void cdcacm_set_config(usbd_device *dev, uint16_t wValue) {
    (void)wValue;
    usbd_ep_setup(dev, 0x01, USB_ENDPOINT_ATTR_BULK, 64,
                  cdcacm_data_rx_cb);
    usbd_ep_setup(dev, 0x82, USB_ENDPOINT_ATTR_BULK, 64, NULL);
    usbd_ep_setup(dev, 0x83, USB_ENDPOINT_ATTR_INTERRUPT, 16, NULL);
}

int main(void) {
    /* Clock: 72 MHz from 8 MHz HSE */
    rcc_clock_setup_pll(&rcc_hse_configs[RCC_CLOCK_HSE8_72MHZ]);

    /* Enable GPIOA and GPIOC clocks (usbd_init enables the USB clock) */
    rcc_periph_clock_enable(RCC_GPIOA);
    rcc_periph_clock_enable(RCC_GPIOC);

    /* User LED on PC13 (active low) */
    gpio_set_mode(GPIOC, GPIO_MODE_OUTPUT_2_MHZ,
                  GPIO_CNF_OUTPUT_PUSHPULL, GPIO13);
    gpio_clear(GPIOC, GPIO13);  /* LED on */

    /* USB D+ (PA12) pull-up handled by hardware resistor R1 */
    usbd_dev = usbd_init(&st_usbfs_v1_usb_driver,
                         &dev_descr,
                         NULL,      /* config descriptor (omitted here) */
                         NULL, 0,   /* strings, number of strings */
                         serial_buf, sizeof(serial_buf));
    usbd_register_set_config_callback(usbd_dev, cdcacm_set_config);

    /* Main loop: poll USB and send message periodically */
    uint32_t count = 0;
    while (1) {
        usbd_poll(usbd_dev);
        count++;
        if (count > 800000) {
            count = 0;
            const char *msg = "Hello from STM32\r\n";
            usbd_ep_write_packet(usbd_dev, 0x82,
                                 msg, strlen(msg));
        }
    }
}
```

**Compile and link:**

```bash
arm-none-eabi-gcc -mcpu=cortex-m3 -mthumb \
    -I libopencm3/include \
    -DSTM32F1 \
    -Os -Wall \
    -T stm32f103c8t6.ld -nostartfiles \
    -L libopencm3/lib \
    -o firmware.elf main.c \
    -lopencm3_stm32f1 -lnosys

arm-none-eabi-objcopy -O binary firmware.elf firmware.bin
```

</TabItem>
</Tabs>
