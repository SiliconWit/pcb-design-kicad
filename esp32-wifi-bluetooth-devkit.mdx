A board with copper under its antenna can lose most of its WiFi range: a link that should reach across a house reaches across one room. The program is correct, so people search the code for days, but the fault is in the copper. A wireless board has layout rules that a purely digital board does not have. In this lesson you design a development board around the ESP32-WROOM-32 radio module, add the circuit that lets a computer program it with one click, and lay out a 2-layer board that keeps the antenna clear and the ground copper unbroken. #KiCad #ESP32 #RFDesign

The drawing shows the one rule that matters most. The antenna end of the module sits at the board edge, and nothing made of copper is allowed under it or beside it.

## What We Are Building

<Card title="ESP32 WiFi/Bluetooth Development Kit" icon="star">
A development board in the style of Espressif's DevKitC, built around the ESP32-WROOM-32 module. A CP2102N USB-UART bridge programs it and carries serial messages, an auto-reset circuit puts the ESP32 into bootloader mode without button presses, and two 19-pin headers bring out all GPIO. Power comes from USB Micro-B through an AMS1117-3.3 LDO.
</Card>

Four terms appear on every page of this lesson:

- A USB-UART bridge is a chip that turns USB into the two-wire serial link (UART) through which the ESP32 is programmed.
- The bootloader is a small program inside the ESP32 that receives new firmware over that serial link.
- An LDO (low dropout regulator) makes a steady 3.3V from the 5V of the USB cable.
- GPIO means a general purpose input or output pin.

## ESP32 Module Overview

<InArticleAd />


The ESP32-WROOM-32 is a module: a small board of its own, soldered onto yours. Under its metal can, Espressif has placed the ESP32 chip, a 40 MHz crystal, 4 MB of flash memory and the small parts they need. The antenna is a copper pattern on the module's own board, and it extends beyond the metal can at one end. That end is the part you must keep clear of copper on your board.

A module saves the hardest work of a radio design: the circuit that matches the chip to the antenna is already tuned, and the module has passed the radio approval tests (FCC, CE and others).

### Key Pins

EN is the reset pin: when it is low the chip is held in reset, and when it rises the chip starts.

| Pin | Function | Notes |
|-----|----------|-------|
| EN | Chip enable | Active high, must have RC filter (10 kΩ + 100 nF) |
| GPIO0 | Boot mode strapping | LOW at reset = bootloader, HIGH = normal boot |
| TXD0 (GPIO1) | UART0 TX | Default serial output, connects to CP2102N RXD |
| RXD0 (GPIO3) | UART0 RX | Default serial input, connects to CP2102N TXD |
| GPIO2 | Strapping pin | Must be LOW or floating during boot. Safe for LED after boot |
| VDD 3.3V | Power supply | Multiple pins, all must be connected |
| GND | Ground | Multiple pins, all must be connected |

### Strapping Pins

A strapping pin is a pin the chip reads once, at the moment it leaves reset, to decide how to start. After that moment it is an ordinary pin. The two that matter on this board:

| Pin | Boot from Flash | Boot to Bootloader |
|-----|----------------|-------------------|
| GPIO0 | HIGH (default pull-up) | LOW (held by BOOT button or auto-reset) |
| GPIO2 | LOW or floating | LOW or floating |

GPIO5, GPIO12 and GPIO15 are strapping pins too. A load that pulls one of the five to the wrong level at reset stops the chip from starting, so read the module datasheet before you connect anything heavy to them.

## RF-Aware Design Principles

<InArticleAd />


RF means radio frequency. An antenna is cut to a size that responds to one frequency, as a guitar string is tuned to one note. WiFi and Bluetooth use 2.4 GHz. Copper close to the antenna acts as a finger resting on the string: the note moves away. The radio then sends less power and hears less, and the range falls. The comparison stops there: a detuned antenna gives no sign you can hear or see, only a weak link.

Four rules follow from this.

1. Keep an antenna keep-out zone. No copper of any kind (no traces, no ground pour, no pads) may lie under the antenna section of the module or within 2mm of it, on any layer.
2. Keep the ground unbroken under the metal can. The radio's return currents flow through the ground copper under the module body. A slot or gap there harms the range as much as copper under the antenna.
3. Put 100 nF decoupling capacitors beside the module's VDD pins. The ESP32 takes current in sudden bursts when it transmits (peaks over 300 mA). A capacitor at the pin supplies the burst. Without it the voltage dips, and the module can reset or send damaged packets.
4. Keep digital signals away from the antenna end. Fast switching signals (an SPI clock, I2C, PWM) radiate noise that the radio receives as interference.

### Antenna Keepout Summary

| Zone | Copper Allowed? | Components Allowed? |
|------|----------------|-------------------|
| Under antenna (beyond metal can) | No, on any layer | No |
| Within 2mm of antenna edges | No, on any layer | No |
| Under metal shielding can | Yes, ground plane required | Decoupling caps on back side OK |
| Board edge near antenna | Board must extend to module edge or beyond | No connectors near antenna end |

## Schematic Capture

<InArticleAd />


Create a new KiCad project named `esp32-devkit`. Open the schematic editor and build the following sub-circuits.

<LinkButton href="https://github.com/SiliconWit/pcb-design-kicad/blob/main/schematics/esp32-wifi-bluetooth-devkit.pdf" target="_blank" variant="secondary" icon="document">Download Schematic PDF</LinkButton>

The PDF is an earlier drawing of this board and differs from the text: its part numbers are different (the transistor on EN is Q1 there), the LED is on GPIO18, the headers have 17 pins, and it has no polyfuse. Where the two differ, follow the text.

### Power Supply

<Steps>
1. Place the USB Micro-B connector. Add `USB_B_Micro` from the Connector library. This provides VBUS (5V), D+, D-, ID, and GND.

2. Add VBUS protection. Connect VBUS through polyfuse F1 (500 mA) to a net labeled `VBUS_5V`. A polyfuse opens when too much current flows and closes again when it cools, so a short circuit on your board cannot harm the computer's USB port. 500 mA is what a standard USB port supplies. Place a 4.7 µF capacitor (C7) from VBUS_5V to GND near the connector.

3. Place the AMS1117-3.3 LDO (SOT-223). Connect VBUS_5V to the input pin, with a 10 µF capacitor (C1) from the input to GND. Connect the output to a `+3V3` power net, with a 10 µF capacitor (C2) from the output to GND. The AMS1117 needs both capacitors to stay stable: without them its output can oscillate.

4. Add power flags. Place `PWR_FLAG` symbols on +3V3 and GND if ERC (the electrical rules check) reports undriven power nets.
</Steps>

### ESP32-WROOM-32 Module

<Steps>
1. Place the ESP32 module. Add `ESP32-WROOM-32` from the RF_Module library. The symbol shows all exposed pins of the module including the ground pad.

2. Connect all power pins. Wire every VDD pin to `+3V3` and every GND pin (including the large ground pad) to `GND`. All the ground pins must be connected, because they carry the radio's return currents.

3. EN pin circuit. Connect EN to +3V3 through a 10 kΩ pull-up resistor (R1). Place a 100 nF capacitor (C6) from EN to GND. The pair is a filter: a short noise pulse cannot reset the module. It also delays the rise of EN by about 1 ms, which the auto-reset circuit needs. The EN pin also connects to the auto-reset circuit and the EN pushbutton (SW1).

4. GPIO0 strapping. Connect GPIO0 to +3V3 through a 10 kΩ pull-up resistor (R2). The pull-up holds GPIO0 high at reset, so the chip starts your program from flash. GPIO0 also connects to the auto-reset circuit and the BOOT pushbutton (SW2).

5. UART0 connections. Connect TXD0 (GPIO1) to the CP2102N RXD pin. Connect RXD0 (GPIO3) to the CP2102N TXD pin. The lines cross: what one chip transmits (TX), the other receives (RX).

6. Decoupling capacitors. Place 100 nF caps (C3, C4) on the VDD pins. Draw them beside the module in the schematic, so that you remember to place them beside it on the board.

7. User LED. Connect GPIO2 through a 330 ohm resistor (R5) to the anode of D1 (blue LED), cathode to GND. The resistor limits the LED current to a few milliamperes. An LED to GND also holds GPIO2 low at reset, which this strapping pin requires.

8. GPIO headers. Place two 1x19 pin headers (J2, J3) and route all remaining GPIO pins to them. Follow the ESP32-DevKitC pinout order if you want compatibility with existing tutorials and shields.
</Steps>

### CP2102N USB-UART Bridge

<Steps>
1. **Place the CP2102N.** Add `CP2102N-Axx-xQFN28` from the Interface_USB library (this symbol covers the CP2102N-A02-GQFN28). The CP2102N makes its own clock, so it needs no crystal.

2. **USB data lines.** Connect the USB Micro-B D+ and D- pins to the CP2102N's D+ and D- pins.

3. **Power connections.** Connect the CP2102N VDD pin to +3V3 and VBUS to the VBUS pin (if available on the package) or to +3V3 through the regulator. Add a 100 nF decoupling capacitor (C5) from VDD to GND.

4. **UART pins.** Connect CP2102N TXD to ESP32 RXD0 (GPIO3). Connect CP2102N RXD to ESP32 TXD0 (GPIO1).

5. **Control signals.** Bring out the RTS and DTR pins. These are two spare outputs of the serial port that the programming tool (esptool, or the Arduino IDE) can switch from the computer. They feed the auto-reset circuit.
</Steps>

### Auto-Reset Circuit

To load new firmware by hand you must hold the BOOT button, press and release EN, release BOOT, and then click Upload, every time. The auto-reset circuit presses the two buttons for you: the programming tool switches DTR and RTS, and two NPN transistors turn those into the right order of events on EN and GPIO0.

An NPN transistor here is a switch. It connects its collector to its emitter only when its base is higher than its emitter. Each transistor has its base on one control line and its emitter on the other:

```text
  DTR ──[R4 10k]──► base    ┐
                            ├─ Q2 ─ collector ──► EN
  RTS ────────────► emitter ┘

  RTS ──[R3 10k]──► base    ┐
                            ├─ Q1 ─ collector ──► GPIO0
  DTR ────────────► emitter ┘
```

So a transistor conducts only when the two lines differ. The table gives the voltage at the CP2102N pins. Both lines rest high, and "asserted" in the tool means the pin is at 0 V.

| DTR pin | RTS pin | Q1 | Q2 | Result |
|---------|---------|----|----|--------|
| High | High | Off | Off | The board runs (the resting state) |
| High | Low | Off | On | EN is low: the chip is held in reset |
| Low | High | On | Off | GPIO0 is low: the bootloader is selected |
| Low | Low | Off | Off | The board runs |

An upload uses rows two, three and one, in that order. RTS low holds the chip in reset. RTS released and DTR low: EN rises, and the chip starts, reads GPIO0 low and enters the bootloader. DTR released: the firmware goes over the UART.

No emitter goes to GND. With grounded emitters, the two lines resting high would switch both transistors on and hold the chip in reset for ever.

<Steps>
1. **Transistor Q1 (controls GPIO0).** Connect the collector to the GPIO0 net. Connect the emitter to the DTR signal from the CP2102N. Connect the base through a 10 kΩ resistor (R3) to the RTS signal.

2. **Transistor Q2 (controls EN).** Connect the collector to the EN net. Connect the emitter to the RTS signal from the CP2102N. Connect the base through a 10 kΩ resistor (R4) to the DTR signal. The 10 kΩ resistors limit the base current to a fraction of a milliampere, which is enough to switch the transistor.

3. **Manual buttons.** Wire SW1 (EN button) between the EN net and GND. Wire SW2 (BOOT button) between GPIO0 and GND. These allow manual reset and bootloader entry, for example if the auto-reset circuit has a fault.
</Steps>

:::note
This is the circuit of Espressif's own ESP32-DevKitC. If you use N-channel MOSFETs (like 2N7002) in place of the NPN transistors, connect the gate where the base would go, the drain to EN or GPIO0, and the source to the other control line (where the emitter would go).
:::

### ERC and Footprint Assignment

<Steps>
1. **Run ERC.** Click Inspect > Electrical Rules Checker. Common warnings to address: unconnected GPIO pins (add "no connect" flags if intentionally unused), missing power flags, and conflicting pin types on the USB data lines.

2. **Assign footprints.** Open Tools > Assign Footprints. Key assignments:
   - ESP32-WROOM-32: `RF_Module:ESP32-WROOM-32` (the official KiCad footprint includes the antenna keepout courtyard)
   - CP2102N: `Package_DFN_QFN:QFN-28-1EP_5x5mm_P0.5mm_EP3.35x3.35mm`
   - AMS1117-3.3: `Package_TO_SOT_SMD:SOT-223-3_TabPin2`
   - USB Micro-B: Select a footprint matching your connector (e.g., `Connector_USB:USB_Micro-B_Molex_47346-0001`)
   - 0402 passives: `Capacitor_SMD:C_0402_1005Metric` and `Resistor_SMD:R_0402_1005Metric`
   - 0805 bulk caps: `Capacitor_SMD:C_0805_2012Metric`
   - Tactile switches: `Button_Switch_SMD:SW_SPST_PTS810`

3. **Generate the netlist.** Click Tools > Update PCB from Schematic (or press F8) to transfer the schematic to the PCB editor.
</Steps>

## PCB Layout

<InArticleAd />


### Board Outline and Module Placement

<Steps>
1. **Draw the board outline.** Switch to the Edge.Cuts layer and draw a rectangle approximately 55mm x 28mm. The ESP32 module is 18mm wide, so 28mm gives adequate clearance on both sides for pin headers.

2. **Place the ESP32 module at one short edge.** Position the module so the antenna section extends to (or slightly beyond) the board edge. The antenna must point outward with no board material or components in front of it. Centre the module across the width of the board.

3. **Place the USB connector at the opposite short edge.** The USB connector and its cable carry digital noise, so they go as far from the antenna as the board allows. Set the connector's metal shell flush with the board edge.

4. **Place the LDO between USB and ESP32.** Position the AMS1117-3.3 and its capacitors along the power path from USB to the module. Keep the power traces short and direct.

5. **Place the CP2102N near the USB connector.** Short USB data lines pick up less noise. Place C5 (decoupling) beside the CP2102N VDD pin.
</Steps>

### Antenna Keepout Zone in KiCad

A keep-out drawn as a Rule Area makes KiCad refuse copper there, so you do not depend on your eyes.

<Steps>
1. **Identify the antenna area.** The ESP32-WROOM-32 footprint in KiCad includes a courtyard (the outline of the space a part needs) that marks the antenna region. The antenna extends approximately 5mm beyond the metal shielding can at the top of the module.

2. **Draw a Rule Area.** Select Add Rule Area (also called Keep Out Zone) from the right toolbar. In the properties dialog, check "Keep out copper pour" for all copper layers (F.Cu, the front, and B.Cu, the back). Draw a rectangle covering the entire antenna area plus 2mm margin on all sides.

3. **Verify no copper exists in the keepout.** After filling zones (press B), look at the antenna area. There should be no ground pour, no traces, and no vias within the keepout rectangle on any copper layer.

4. **Board edge alignment.** If the antenna extends beyond the board edge, the Edge.Cuts outline should follow the module edge at the antenna end. Some designers extend the board a little beyond the antenna to protect it, and leave that part free of copper.
</Steps>

### Ground Pour and Plane Strategy

A ground pour is copper that fills all the free space of a layer and is connected to GND. A 2-layer board has no inner layer to give to ground, so you pour ground on both F.Cu and B.Cu. The pour must be solid and unbroken under the metal can of the module, and absent under the antenna.

On F.Cu the pour fills around the pads and traces. On B.Cu, keep it as continuous as you can, most of all under the module. Join the two pours with vias (plated holes) every 3 to 5mm. This spacing is small against the wavelength of a 2.4 GHz signal in the board, about 60mm, so to the radio the two layers act as one sheet of ground.

<Steps>
1. **Create the GND zones.** Add a filled zone covering the entire board on F.Cu, assigned to the GND net, with priority 0. Do the same on B.Cu.

2. **Add the vias.** Place GND vias every 3 to 5mm across the board (not in the antenna keepout zone), with more around the module's ground pad and along the board edges.

3. **Verify ground continuity.** After filling zones, use the net inspector to confirm that all GND pads are on one net. Look for islands of ground that a trace has cut off from the pour.
</Steps>

### Placing the Remaining Parts

| Component | Placement Rule |
|-----------|---------------|
| Auto-reset transistors | Between the CP2102N and the ESP32 EN/GPIO0 pins |
| Decoupling caps (C3, C4) | Beside the ESP32 VDD pins, back side OK |
| Buttons (EN, BOOT) | Board edge, where a finger can reach |
| GPIO headers | Both long edges of the board |

### Trace Routing Tips

Route in this order:

1. The USB D+ and D- traces, short and side by side between the Micro-B connector and the CP2102N. These are USB 2.0 Full-Speed (12 Mbps) signals: keep them under 50mm and avoid sharp 90-degree bends.
2. The UART lines (TXD, RXD) between the CP2102N and the ESP32 module. At up to 921600 baud they are slow, so their exact width does not matter. Keep them away from the antenna area.
3. The power traces (VBUS to LDO input, LDO output to the 3V3 net), 0.5mm wide or more. The ESP32 can take up to 500 mA from the 3.3V rail when it transmits, and a narrow trace loses voltage and warms.
4. The remaining GPIO connections to the pin headers, away from the antenna end of the board.

### Design Rules for JLCPCB 2-Layer

A design rule is a limit the factory can make reliably. KiCad checks your board against the limits you enter. The minimum column is what JLCPCB can make, and the recommended column leaves a margin. Set them in Board Setup > Design Rules > Constraints:

| Parameter | Minimum | Recommended |
|-----------|---------|-------------|
| Trace width | 0.127 mm | 0.25 mm (0.5 mm for power) |
| Trace spacing | 0.127 mm | 0.2 mm |
| Via drill | 0.3 mm | 0.3 mm |
| Via diameter | 0.6 mm | 0.6 mm |
| Copper to edge | 0.2 mm | 0.3 mm |

### Running DRC

DRC is the design rules check: KiCad compares the board with the limits above.

<Steps>
1. Fill all zones (Edit > Fill All Zones, or press B).

2. Open Inspect > Design Rules Checker.

3. Fix any clearance violations, unconnected nets, or minimum width violations.

4. Look closely at the antenna keepout zone. DRC must report no copper inside it.
</Steps>

## Manufacturing

<InArticleAd />


A factory works from Gerber files (one drawing for each layer) and a drill file. Generate Gerbers for F.Cu, B.Cu, F.Mask, B.Mask, F.SilkS, B.SilkS, and Edge.Cuts, plus the drill file. Zip everything and upload to JLCPCB. The export steps, the ordering process and the BOM and pick-and-place files are the same as for the [STM32 USB development board](/education/pcb-design-kicad/stm32-usb-development-board/).

For a 2-layer board this size (55x28mm), expect to pay 2 to 5 USD for 5 boards at JLCPCB. Select 1.6mm board thickness and HASL surface finish. If you plan to hand-solder the QFN-28 CP2102N, the ENIG finish gives flatter pads, but HASL works with care. The ESP32 module has castellated pads (half holes on its edges), so a standard iron can solder it: apply flux and solder each pad in turn.

## Firmware: WiFi Scan

<InArticleAd />


When the board is assembled, a network scan shows that the ESP32 starts and that the radio works. The sketch uses the Arduino framework, which hides the setup of Espressif's own software kit.

### Arduino IDE Setup

<Steps>
1. **Install ESP32 board support.** In Arduino IDE, go to File > Preferences and add `https://espressif.github.io/arduino-esp32/package_esp32_index.json` to the Additional Board Manager URLs. Then open Tools > Board > Boards Manager, search for "esp32", and install the Espressif package.

2. **Select the board.** Go to Tools > Board > ESP32 Arduino and select "ESP32 Dev Module".

3. **Select the port.** Plug in the board via USB. The CP2102N should enumerate as a serial port. On Linux it appears as `/dev/ttyUSB0`, on Windows as a COM port, and on macOS as `/dev/tty.usbserial-XXXX`. Select it under Tools > Port.

4. **Set upload speed.** Under Tools > Upload Speed, select 921600 for fast programming. The auto-reset circuit handles the bootloader entry automatically.
</Steps>

### WiFi Scan Sketch

```cpp
/*
 * ESP32 WiFi Scanner
 * Scans for nearby WiFi networks and prints results over serial.
 * Upload via USB with auto-reset (just click Upload in Arduino IDE).
 */

#include <WiFi.h>

void setup() {
    Serial.begin(115200);
    delay(1000);
    Serial.println("ESP32 WiFi Scanner");
    Serial.println("==================");

    WiFi.mode(WIFI_STA);
    WiFi.disconnect();
    delay(100);

    Serial.println("Setup complete. Starting scan...\n");
}

void loop() {
    Serial.println("Scanning for networks...");
    int numNetworks = WiFi.scanNetworks();

    if (numNetworks == 0) {
        Serial.println("No networks found.");
    } else {
        Serial.print(numNetworks);
        Serial.println(" networks found:\n");
        Serial.println("  # | SSID                             | RSSI | Ch | Encryption");
        Serial.println("----+----------------------------------+------+----+-----------");

        for (int i = 0; i < numNetworks; i++) {
            char line[100];
            const char* encType;

            switch (WiFi.encryptionType(i)) {
                case WIFI_AUTH_OPEN:            encType = "Open";     break;
                case WIFI_AUTH_WEP:             encType = "WEP";      break;
                case WIFI_AUTH_WPA_PSK:         encType = "WPA";      break;
                case WIFI_AUTH_WPA2_PSK:        encType = "WPA2";     break;
                case WIFI_AUTH_WPA_WPA2_PSK:    encType = "WPA/WPA2"; break;
                case WIFI_AUTH_WPA2_ENTERPRISE: encType = "WPA2-Ent"; break;
                default:                        encType = "Unknown";  break;
            }

            snprintf(line, sizeof(line), "%3d | %-32s | %4d | %2d | %s",
                     i + 1,
                     WiFi.SSID(i).c_str(),
                     WiFi.RSSI(i),
                     WiFi.channel(i),
                     encType);
            Serial.println(line);
        }
    }

    Serial.println("\nNext scan in 10 seconds...\n");
    delay(10000);
}
```

### Troubleshooting

If the board does not respond after clicking Upload:

- Check the auto-reset circuit. Hold the BOOT button, press and release EN, then release BOOT. Click Upload again. If this works but auto-reset does not, inspect the transistor wiring and the DTR/RTS connections from the CP2102N.
- Measure the 3.3V supply on the ESP32 VDD pins. It should be 3.3V plus or minus 0.1V. Below 3.0V, suspect the LDO or its capacitors.
- If no serial port appears when you plug in the board, look for solder bridges between the pins of the CP2102N.
- If the scan finds no networks, check the antenna keepout zone. Copper under the antenna is the most common cause of poor WiFi on custom ESP32 boards. Check also that no metal box and no hand covers the antenna.
