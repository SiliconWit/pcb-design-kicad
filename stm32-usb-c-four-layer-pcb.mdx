On a two-layer board, ground is the copper that is left between the tracks. Each signal must find its way back to its source around gaps and other tracks, so the board radiates noise, and a USB link that works on your desk can fail with another cable or computer. A board with four copper layers removes the problem: one inner layer is a whole sheet of ground and another is a whole sheet of 3.3V. In this lesson you design a Black Pill style board around the STM32F411CEU6, a 100 MHz microcontroller (MCU), with a USB-C connector for power and data, and you route its USB tracks over the ground sheet. #KiCad #STM32 #FourLayerPCB

The order of the copper sheets and the insulation between them, with every thickness, is called the stack-up. A whole sheet of copper on one layer is called a plane:

```text
  Layer 1  F.Cu     signals and parts
  ═══════════════════════════════════════
           prepreg  about 0.2 mm
  ═══════════════════════════════════════
  Layer 2  In1.Cu   GND plane, whole sheet
  ───────────────────────────────────────
           core     about 1.0 mm
  ───────────────────────────────────────
  Layer 3  In2.Cu   +3V3 plane
  ═══════════════════════════════════════
           prepreg  about 0.2 mm
  ═══════════════════════════════════════
  Layer 4  B.Cu     signals and parts

  Total thickness: about 1.6 mm
```

The core is a rigid sheet of glass fibre and resin with copper on both faces. Prepreg is the same material, pressed in as a thin glue layer.

## Why Four Layers?

<InArticleAd />


Two layers are enough for a slow microcontroller with simple power needs. USB, faster clocks and chips with many close pins are easier and safer on four layers, for four reasons.

The first reason is the return path. Every signal is a loop: current leaves the chip along a track and must come back, and a fast signal comes back by the path that encloses the least area. With a ground plane on Layer 2, that path lies in the plane directly under the track, 0.2 mm away. A small loop radiates and picks up little, so the board makes less electromagnetic interference (EMI). On two layers the return current goes around gaps and other tracks, and the loop is large.

The second reason is power. Layer 3 is a sheet of 3.3V. Each part reaches it through a via (a plated hole that joins layers) beside its pin, so no long, thin power track loses voltage on the way.

The third reason is space. Power and ground are inside the board, so both outer layers are free for signals.

The fourth reason is a known impedance for the USB pair. To a fast signal, a track over a plane has a characteristic impedance, in ohms: the ratio of voltage to current of a wave that travels along it. It depends only on the cross-section: the track width, the height above the plane and the insulation. Where the impedance changes, part of the signal reflects, as a wave on a rope reflects where the rope becomes thicker. USB cables and chips are made for 90 Ω between D+ and D-, so the tracks should continue at that value. Four layers give the small, fixed height above ground that reaches 90 Ω with tracks of normal width.

The price is money: five boards of this size cost about 8 to 15 USD on four layers, against 2 to 5 USD on two.

## USB-C Basics for Device Mode

<InArticleAd />


A USB-C plug fits either way up, and that costs pins: the full connector has 24. A USB 2.0 device uses few of them, so this board takes the 16-pin receptacle. (A 6-pin USB-C receptacle carries power only and has no data pins.) In this section the host is the computer, hub or charger at the other end of the cable.

### Key Signals

| Pin(s) | Signal | Purpose |
|--------|--------|---------|
| A4, A9, B4, B9 | VBUS | 5V power from host |
| A1, A12, B1, B12 | GND | Ground connections |
| A6, A7 | D+, D- | USB 2.0 data pair (side A) |
| B6, B7 | D+, D- | USB 2.0 data pair (side B) |
| A5 | CC1 | Configuration channel 1 |
| B5 | CC2 | Configuration channel 2 |

### CC Resistors

A USB-C host gives no 5V until it knows that something is attached. The two CC (Configuration Channel) pins tell it. A device pulls CC1 and CC2 to GND, each through its own 5.1 kΩ resistor, the value the USB-C standard fixes. The host has a pull-up on the same wire, and the voltage that results tells it that a device is present. The size of the host's pull-up tells the device how much current it may take: 500 mA by default, 1.5 A or 3.0 A if the host offers more.

Without these resistors a USB-C host supplies no power. One resistor shared by both CC pins is a known mistake: the board then fails with some cables and chargers.

### D+ Pull-Up

A full-speed device (12 Mbit/s) announces itself with a 1.5 kΩ pull-up from D+ to 3.3V. The STM32F411 has this pull-up inside and firmware switches it on, so the external resistor R3 is optional. If you fit it, put a solder jumper in series, so you can disconnect it when you use the internal one.

### Connecting Both Orientations

The cable can be plugged in either way up, so the receptacle has D+ and D- on both of its rows. Join A6 to B6 (D+) and A7 to B7 (D-) on the board, and the data lines reach the MCU in either position. Some 16-pin receptacles join them inside: check the data sheet of yours.

## Four-Layer Stackup Design

<InArticleAd />


You do not choose the thicknesses yourself. The factory offers a few stack-ups, and you copy the one you order into KiCad. A common one for a 1.6mm board:

| Layer | Name | Thickness | Content |
|-------|------|-----------|---------|
| 1 | F.Cu | 35 µm (1 oz) | Signal traces, component pads |
| - | Prepreg | ~0.2mm | Dielectric between L1 and L2 |
| 2 | In1.Cu | 17.5 µm (0.5 oz) | GND plane (solid, unbroken) |
| - | Core | ~1.0mm | Rigid FR4 core |
| 3 | In2.Cu | 17.5 µm (0.5 oz) | +3V3 power plane |
| - | Prepreg | ~0.2mm | Dielectric between L3 and L4 |
| 4 | B.Cu | 35 µm (1 oz) | Signal traces, some components |

### What sets the impedance of the pair

Three dimensions set the impedance between the two USB tracks. In each, more copper closer together gives a lower impedance:

- Track width $w$: a wider track gives a lower impedance.
- Height $h$ above the ground plane, here the prepreg of about 0.2 mm: a closer plane gives a lower impedance.
- Gap $s$ between the two tracks: a smaller gap gives a lower impedance.

The insulation counts too. FR4, the glass and resin of the board, has a relative permittivity $\varepsilon_r$ of about 4.3.

A published estimate (IPC-2141) joins these. $Z_0$ is the impedance of one track alone and $t$ is the copper thickness, with all lengths in the same unit:

$$
Z_0 = \frac{87}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r + 1.41}} \ln\!\left(\frac{5.98\,h}{0.8\,w + t}\right), \qquad Z_{\text{diff}} = 2 Z_0 \left(1 - 0.48\, e^{-0.96\, s/h}\right)
$$

For this board's starting values, $w$ = 0.2 mm, $s$ = 0.15 mm, $h$ = 0.21 mm and $t$ = 0.035 mm, it gives $Z_0$ = 68 Ω and $Z_{\text{diff}}$ = 103 Ω. USB allows 90 Ω ± 15%, which is 76.5 to 103.5 Ω, so these values are at the upper edge.

Use the formula for direction and rough size only. For bare tracks it is within about 12% of an exact field calculation, and the solder mask over the tracks lowers the real value by a further 3 to 13 Ω. The factory's own impedance calculator knows its real materials, and its answer is the one to draw.

### Setting Up 4 Layers in KiCad

<Steps>
1. Open **Board Setup** (File > Board Setup, or the gear icon in the PCB editor).

2. Go to **Board Stackup** > **Physical Stackup**. Change the layer count from 2 to 4. KiCad adds In1.Cu and In2.Cu between the existing front and back copper layers.

3. Name the layers. In **Board Stackup** > **Board Editor Layers**, you can rename In1.Cu to "GND" and In2.Cu to "PWR". The name is only a label: a layer carries a net when you fill a zone on it, later.

4. Under **Physical Stackup**, enter the factory's prepreg and core thicknesses, because the impedance is worked out from them. For JLCPCB's standard 4-layer process: prepreg 0.2104mm (a glass cloth called 7628), core 1.065mm.

5. Go to **Design Rules** > **Net Classes**. A net class is a set of rules that KiCad applies to every net you put in it. Create a class called "USB" with track width 0.2mm and differential pair gap 0.15mm, then replace these with the factory calculator's values for 90 Ω. Keep the Default class at 0.25mm track width.
</Steps>

### USB-C Connector

<Steps>
1. **Place the USB-C connector.** Add `USB_C_Receptacle_USB2.0_16P` from the Connector library. This symbol has only the pins USB 2.0 needs: VBUS, GND, D+, D-, CC1, CC2, and shield.

2. **Wire the CC resistors.** Connect one 5.1 kΩ resistor from CC1 to GND (R1) and one from CC2 to GND (R2).

3. **Wire VBUS.** Connect VBUS through polyfuse F1 to a net labeled `VBUS_5V`. A polyfuse is a fuse that resets itself: if the board shorts, it limits the current taken from the computer's port. Add a 10 µF capacitor (C12) from VBUS_5V to GND.

4. **Wire the data lines.** Connect D+ to a net labeled `USB_D+` and D- to a net labeled `USB_D-`. They go to PA12 (D+) and PA11 (D-) on the STM32. The names matter: KiCad's differential pair router finds the partner of a net only when the two names end in `+` and `-`, or `P` and `N`. With `USB_DP` and `USB_DM` it refuses to route them as a pair.

5. **Shield pin.** Connect the metal shell of the connector to GND.

6. **Static protection.** A USB-C socket is touched by people and cables many times a day, and a static spark enters through it. On a board that leaves your desk, add an ESD protection array such as the USBLC6-2SC6 beside the connector, on D+, D- and VBUS, before the tracks reach the MCU.
</Steps>

### Power Supply

<Steps>
1. **Place the LDO.** Add `ME6211C33M5` from the Regulator_Linear library. Its input is VBUS_5V and its output is +3V3. Connect the enable pin to VBUS_5V, so the regulator is on whenever USB is plugged in.

2. **Input capacitor.** C12 (10 µF) on VBUS_5V also serves as the input capacitor. Keep it close to the LDO VIN pin.

3. **Output capacitors.** Place 10 µF (C13) and 100 nF (C14) from +3V3 to GND. The regulator needs at least 1 µF at its output to stay stable, and 10 µF gives a reserve for sudden current steps. The "500 mA" of the ME6211 is its current limit, not what its package can shed as heat. It loses (5 − 3.3) V × current: 0.17 W at 100 mA, 0.85 W at 500 mA. A SOT-23-5 package rises about 250 °C per watt on a small board (typical; check the data sheet), so plan for under about 150 mA (0.26 W).

4. **Power flags.** If the electrical rules check (ERC) reports a power pin that nothing drives, add `PWR_FLAG` symbols on the +3V3 and GND nets. The flag tells KiCad that power enters there.
</Steps>

### STM32F411CEU6

<Steps>
1. **Place the MCU.** Add `STM32F411CEUx` from the MCU_ST_STM32F4 library. This symbol includes all 48 pins plus the exposed thermal pad.

2. **Decoupling capacitors.** A fast chip takes its current in short pulses, and a small capacitor beside each supply pin delivers them. The STM32F411 UFQFPN-48 has three VDD pins and one VDDA pin (the supply of its analog parts), so place four 100 nF capacitors (C5 through C8). Add one more 100 nF (C9) near VDDA with a 1 µF in parallel for analog filtering, and a 4.7 µF bulk capacitor (C10) near the MCU. Connect VBAT (pin 1), the backup supply of the real-time clock, to +3V3 with its own 100 nF beside it: the data sheet asks for this when no battery is fitted, and a VBAT pin left open is a fault.

3. **VCAP1 pin.** The chip makes its own core voltage with an internal regulator, and VCAP1 is that regulator's output. It needs a 4.7 µF ceramic capacitor to GND, the data sheet value for a package with one VCAP pin. Connect nothing else to this pin.

4. **HSE crystal.** HSE is the high-speed external clock. Connect a 25 MHz crystal between OSC_IN (PH0) and OSC_OUT (PH1), with a 10 pF load capacitor from each crystal pin to GND. A crystal runs at its marked frequency only when it sees its specified load capacitance $C_L$. The two capacitors act in series and the board adds about 5 pF of stray capacitance, so each capacitor is $2\,(C_L - 5\,\text{pF})$. For this crystal, $2 \times (10 - 5) = 10\,\text{pF}$.

5. **LSE crystal.** LSE is the low-speed external clock, for the real-time clock. Connect a 32.768 kHz crystal between OSC32_IN (PC14) and OSC32_OUT (PC15), with a 6.8 pF load capacitor from each crystal pin to GND.

6. **Reset circuit.** NRST restarts the chip when it is pulled to 0 V. Connect a 10 kΩ pull-up from NRST to 3V3 and a tactile switch (SW2) from NRST to GND. A 100 nF capacitor from NRST to GND absorbs noise spikes.

7. **BOOT0 pin.** Connect BOOT0 through a 10 kΩ pull-down to GND, so the MCU starts your program from its flash memory. For a BOOT0 button, add a switch from BOOT0 to 3V3.

8. **USB data lines.** Connect PA11 to `USB_D-` and PA12 to `USB_D+`. If you fit the external pull-up, connect the 1.5 kΩ resistor from `USB_D+` to +3V3.
</Steps>

### SWD Header and User I/O

<Steps>
1. **SWD header.** SWD (Serial Wire Debug) is the two-wire connection through which a debugger loads and tests a program. Place a 1x5 pin header. Wire pin 1 to +3V3, pin 2 to SWDIO (PA13), pin 3 to SWCLK (PA14), pin 4 to GND, pin 5 to NRST. An ST-Link V2 and other SWD debuggers connect to it.

2. **User LED.** Connect +3V3 to a green LED, then a 330 Ω resistor, then PC13. The LED lights when PC13 is low, as on the Black Pill. The resistor limits the LED current to a few milliamperes.

3. **User button.** Connect PA0 to GND through a tactile switch (SW1). Enable the internal pull-up in firmware, or add an external 10 kΩ pull-up to +3V3.

4. **GPIO headers.** Place two 1x20 headers along the board edges and connect all remaining GPIO pins to them. Follow the Black Pill pin order if you want your board to replace one.
</Steps>

### ERC and Footprint Assignment

<Steps>
1. **Run ERC.** Click Inspect > Electrical Rules Checker. Correct every unconnected pin, conflict between pin types and missing power flag.

2. **Assign footprints.** Open Tools > Assign Footprints. Key assignments:
   - STM32F411CEU6: `Package_DFN_QFN:QFN-48-1EP_7x7mm_P0.5mm_EP5.6x5.6mm` (the UFQFPN-48 footprint with exposed pad)
   - USB-C: Select a footprint matching your connector (e.g., `Connector_USB:USB_C_Receptacle_HRO_TYPE-C-31-M-12`)
   - ME6211: `Package_TO_SOT_SMD:SOT-23-5`
   - 0402 passives: `Capacitor_SMD:C_0402_1005Metric` and `Resistor_SMD:R_0402_1005Metric`
   - 0805 bulk caps: `Capacitor_SMD:C_0805_2012Metric`

3. **Exposed pad.** The UFQFPN-48 exposed pad must connect to GND for thermal and electrical performance. In the schematic, the exposed pad pin should already be connected to GND. Verify this in the footprint properties.
</Steps>

## PCB Layout

<InArticleAd />


### Board Outline and Zones

<Steps>
1. **Draw the board outline.** Switch to the Edge.Cuts layer and draw a rectangle 55mm x 20mm. Round the corners with 1mm radius fillets if desired.

2. **Create the GND zone on In1.Cu.** A zone is an area that KiCad fills with copper for one net. Use **Place > Draw Filled Zones**, pick the In1.Cu layer, and assign it to the GND net. Draw the zone over the whole board. This is the ground plane.

3. **Create the +3V3 zone on In2.Cu.** Do the same on In2.Cu, assigned to the +3V3 net. This is the power plane.

4. **Create a GND pour on F.Cu and B.Cu.** Add filled zones on both outer layers, assigned to GND. Vias join them to the inner GND plane, and they shield the signals beside them.
</Steps>

### Component Placement

Place the USB-C connector at one short edge of the board and the MCU in the center, on the front side. Place the LDO and its capacitors between the two, so the power path is short.

Place each decoupling capacitor on the back of the board, directly under its VDD pin. Only a four-layer board allows this: one via joins the capacitor to the GND plane (In1.Cu) and another joins it to the +3V3 plane (In2.Cu), so the path of the current pulse is a few millimetres long.

Place the HSE crystal within 5mm of the OSC_IN/OSC_OUT pins and the LSE crystal within 3mm of the OSC32 pins. A long crystal track adds stray capacitance and picks up noise, so keep fast digital tracks away from both.

Place the SWD header at the short edge opposite USB, or along a long edge. Place the GPIO headers along both long edges.

Place the parts in this order, because the first ones have the least freedom: USB-C connector, MCU, crystals, decoupling capacitors, LDO, SWD header, GPIO headers.

### Via Stitching

The GND pours on the outer layers help only where they are joined to the GND plane, at many points; a piece of pour joined at one point acts as an antenna. Via stitching is a regular pattern of GND vias that makes those joints. It also carries heat from the outer layers to the inner copper.

<Steps>
1. **Stitch around the board edge.** Use **Place > Place Vias** and check in each via's properties that its net is GND. Place vias every 3 to 5mm around the board, inside the ground pour, at least 0.5mm from the edge.

2. **Stitch beside the USB tracks.** Place GND vias on both sides of the USB differential pair, every 2 to 3mm, so the pours beside the pair are firmly at ground.

3. **Stitch near the MCU.** Place GND vias around the MCU footprint. Under the chip, its exposed pad gets a grid of 4 to 9 vias (2x2 or 3x3) to the inner GND plane, which carry heat away from the chip.

4. **Faster stitching.** KiCad 9 has no via stitching command in its menus. Place one GND via, select it, and use **Create Array...** (`Ctrl+T`) for a row or grid at 3 mm spacing, or install a via stitching plugin from the **Plugin and Content Manager**. Use vias of 0.45mm diameter and 0.2mm drill.
</Steps>

### USB Differential Pair Routing

The USB pair is routed first, before other tracks take its space.

<Steps>
1. **Assign the USB net class.** In Board Setup > Net Classes, check that `USB_D+` and `USB_D-` are in the "USB" net class, with the track width and gap from the factory's calculator for 90 Ω on your stack-up. The starting values of 0.2mm and 0.15mm on 0.21mm prepreg are inside the USB range, at its upper edge.

2. **Route the differential pair.** Open **Route > Route Differential Pair** (or press `6`). Click one pad of the D+/D- pair at the connector, and KiCad draws both tracks together and holds the gap between them.

3. **Keep the pair on one layer.** Route the whole pair on F.Cu, with no vias. A via changes the impedance at that point, and it moves the signal away from its ground plane.

4. **Match the lengths.** Make D+ and D- equal within 0.15mm, so the two halves of each bit arrive together. KiCad's length tuning tool (in the Route menu) adds small bends to the shorter track.

5. **Keep the ground plane whole.** Route no track on In1.Cu under the USB pair. A slot in the plane forces the return current around it: the loop grows and the impedance changes at the slot. If another signal must cross the pair, cross on B.Cu.

6. **Termination.** The STM32's USB driver already has the right output resistance, so no series resistors are needed. If the signal shows ringing on an oscilloscope, add 22 Ω in series with D+ and D-.
</Steps>

A 12 Mbit/s signal on 20 mm of track is forgiving, and a board with a wrong pair impedance usually works. The method matters because it is the same for faster links, where a wrong value stops the link.

### Decoupling Capacitor Routing

Connect each decoupling capacitor through vias, not through tracks: one via from its power pad to the +3V3 plane (In2.Cu) and one from its ground pad to the GND plane (In1.Cu). The loop is then the distance between the two vias times the board thickness, which is very small. If a capacitor cannot sit directly under its VDD pin, keep its track as short as you can and still use both vias.

### Design Rules for JLCPCB 4-Layer

Set these minimum design rules in Board Setup > Design Rules > Constraints:

| Parameter | Minimum | Recommended |
|-----------|---------|-------------|
| Trace width | 0.09 mm | 0.15 mm (0.2 mm for USB) |
| Trace spacing | 0.09 mm | 0.15 mm |
| Via drill | 0.15 mm | 0.2 mm |
| Via diameter | 0.35 mm | 0.45 mm |
| Annular ring | 0.075 mm | 0.1 mm |
| Copper to edge | 0.2 mm | 0.3 mm |

The minimum is what the factory can make. The recommended value leaves a margin, so a small error in manufacture does not break a track. Check the factory's capabilities page before you finish: these limits change.

### Running DRC

The design rules check (DRC) compares the layout with these rules and with the schematic.

<Steps>
1. Fill all zones (Edit > Fill All Zones, or press B).

2. Open Inspect > Design Rules Checker.

3. Correct every clearance violation, unconnected net and track that is too narrow.

4. Look at In1.Cu alone. DRC accepts a track on this layer, but a track there cuts a slot in the GND plane. Move any track you find to an outer layer.
</Steps>

## Manufacturing

<InArticleAd />


You export Gerber and drill files as for the [STM32 USB development board](/education/pcb-design-kicad/stm32-usb-development-board/). Only the points that differ for four layers are given here.

### 4-Layer Options at JLCPCB

When you order, select "4" for the layer count and the standard stack-up JLC04161H-7628, the one you entered in KiCad.

### Impedance Control

With impedance control selected in the order form, the factory measures and adjusts its process to reach your target within about 10%. This adds roughly 20 to 40 USD. For full-speed USB the standard stack-up is close enough, so the option is not needed here.

### Gerber Export Checklist

A four-layer order needs two more copper files than a two-layer order, one for each inner layer:

| File | KiCad Layer |
|------|-------------|
| `project-F_Cu.gtl` | F.Cu (Layer 1, top signals) |
| `project-In1_Cu.g2` | In1.Cu (Layer 2, GND plane) |
| `project-In2_Cu.g3` | In2.Cu (Layer 3, power plane) |
| `project-B_Cu.gbl` | B.Cu (Layer 4, bottom signals) |
| F.Mask, B.Mask | Solder masks |
| F.SilkS, B.SilkS | Silkscreen |
| Edge.Cuts | Board outline |

Zip all Gerber and drill files together and upload them. In the factory's Gerber viewer, check that the inner layers are in the right order before you pay: if they are exchanged, the USB pair lies over the power plane.

### Check before you order

Do the six checks of the [breakout board](/education/pcb-design-kicad/atmega328p-breakout-board-through-hole/#check-before-you-order), and four more for this board:

- **Zones filled.** Fill all zones (`B`) before you plot; an unfilled plane is plotted as an empty layer. The plot dialog's **Check zone fills before plotting** does it for you.
- **The planes in the Gerber viewer:** In1.Cu a whole sheet of GND with round clearances around the +3V3 vias and no slot under the USB pair, In2.Cu the same for +3V3.
- **The exposed pad:** GND, with its vias, in the footprint and on the paste layer.
- **USB-C:** each CC pin has its own 5.1 kΩ to GND, and A6/B6 and A7/B7 are joined.

## Firmware: USB CDC Virtual COM Port

<InArticleAd />


A wiring fault on a USB board can damage the board and the port that feeds it. Before first power, look for solder bridges, and check with a meter that no two of VBUS_5V, +3V3 and GND are joined. Power the new board from a USB charger or a hub first, not from a computer, and measure 3.25 to 3.35V on +3V3. If a part becomes hot, unplug at once.

Then prove the board by sending text to the computer over USB. The STM32F411 has full-speed USB on PA11 and PA12.

### STM32CubeMX Configuration

<Steps>
1. Open STM32CubeMX and create a new project for the STM32F411CEU6.

2. Under **System Core > RCC**, set High Speed Clock (HSE) to "Crystal/Ceramic Resonator" and Low Speed Clock (LSE) to "Crystal/Ceramic Resonator".

3. Under **Connectivity > USB_OTG_FS**, set Mode to "Device_Only".

4. Under **Middleware > USB_DEVICE**, set Class to "Communication Device Class (Virtual Port Com)".

5. In the **Clock Configuration** tab, set the system clock to 96 MHz from the PLL (a circuit that multiplies a clock to a higher frequency), with HSE as its source. USB needs exactly 48 MHz, and 96 MHz divides to it by 2.

6. Generate the project code for your toolchain (STM32CubeIDE, Makefile, or CMake).
</Steps>

### Sending Data Over USB

In the generated project, the function `CDC_Transmit_FS()` in `usbd_cdc_if.c` sends data to the host. Add this test in `main.c`:

```c
/* main.c (inside the while(1) loop in main()) */
#include "usbd_cdc_if.h"

/* In main(), after MX_USB_DEVICE_Init() */
uint8_t msg[] = "STM32F411 Black Pill alive\r\n";
uint32_t tick = 0;

while (1)
{
    if (HAL_GetTick() - tick >= 1000)
    {
        tick = HAL_GetTick();
        CDC_Transmit_FS(msg, sizeof(msg) - 1);
    }
}
```

Load the program through SWD with an ST-Link or J-Link. Connect the board to a computer over USB-C, and a virtual COM port appears. Open a serial terminal at any baud rate (a USB serial port ignores the setting) and you see:

```
STM32F411 Black Pill alive
STM32F411 Black Pill alive
STM32F411 Black Pill alive
```

On Linux, the device appears as `/dev/ttyACM0`. On Windows, it appears as a COM port in Device Manager. On macOS, look for `/dev/tty.usbmodemXXXX`.

### Troubleshooting USB Enumeration

If the device does not appear on the host:

- No power: measure VBUS. It must be 5V with the cable connected. With a cable that has USB-C at both ends, 0V points to the 5.1 kΩ resistors on CC1 and CC2. Measure the CC pins with the cable in: one of them carries the host's offer across your 5.1 kΩ, about 0.42 V for default USB power, 0.94 V for 1.5 A and 1.69 V for 3 A (host pull-ups of 56, 22 and 10 kΩ to 5 V). This reading proves the resistors and tells you what the port offers.
- Power but no USB device, and the program runs: in CubeMX, keep VBUS sensing disabled. This board does not connect VBUS to PA9, and with sensing on the chip waits for a VBUS it never sees.
- Power but no device: the host looks for D+ held high. With the internal pull-up, the program must start USB, so check that it runs.
- "Unknown device": USB needs exactly 48 MHz. Check the clock tree in CubeMX and the 25 MHz crystal.
- Random faults: check the 4.7 µF capacitor on VCAP1. Without it the core regulator is unstable.
- Still nothing: change the cable. Some USB-C cables carry power only.
