An ESP32 development board takes 40 to 80 mA when it does nothing, and that empties a small lithium cell in less than a day. A sensor that must work in a field for months needs a board of its own, where every part is chosen for the current it takes while the node sleeps, and where the cell is charged and protected safely. In this lesson you design that board around the ESP32-C3-MINI-1 module, with a lithium charger, solar input, battery protection, and a power budget of months on one cell. #KiCad #ESP32 #IoT

The energy goes from two sources, through the cell, to two loads:

```text
  Solar panel ──►┐
  (5V)           │
                 ▼
  USB-C (5V) ──► TP4056 ──► protection
                 (charger)  (DW01A + 8205A)
                     │           │
                     ▼           ▼
                 lithium cell (3.7V, 500mAh)
                     │
                     ▼
                 ME6211 LDO ──► 3.3V ──► ESP32-C3
                 (regulator)        └──► BME280
```

## What We Are Building

<Card title="Battery-Powered ESP32-C3 IoT Sensor Node" icon="star">
A wireless sensor node that measures temperature, humidity and air pressure with a BME280. The ESP32-C3 wakes from deep sleep every 10 minutes, reads the sensor, sends the data over WiFi or BLE, and sleeps again. A TP4056 charges the lithium cell from USB-C or a small solar panel. The board takes about 50 µA while it sleeps.
</Card>

Three terms:

- In deep sleep the ESP32 switches off almost everything and takes a few microamperes, until a timer wakes it.
- Quiescent current (Iq) is the current a part takes for itself when its load takes nothing.
- An LDO (low dropout regulator) makes a steady 3.3V from the cell, whose voltage falls from 4.2V to 3.0V as it empties.

## Power System Design

<InArticleAd />


Each stage of the power system also takes a little current for itself, all day, and those small currents decide how long the node sleeps on one charge.

:::caution[Lithium cells]
A lithium cell can catch fire if it is short-circuited, overcharged or connected backwards. Use a cell with its own protection circuit. Sellers do not all wire this type of connector the same way, so check the polarity with a meter before you connect a cell. Stay with the board for its first charge, and stop if the cell or the charger becomes hot. This board uses 5V sources only, never mains.
:::

### Battery Charging: TP4056

A lithium cell is charged in two phases. First the charger holds the current constant while the cell voltage rises. At 4.2V it holds the voltage constant, and the current falls by itself. Above 4.2V the cell is damaged. The TP4056 does both phases and ends the charge when the current has fallen to about 1/10 of the set value.

Key connections:

- PROG: a resistor to GND that sets the charge current.
- VIN: the USB-C VBUS or the solar panel, through the diode-OR network.
- BAT: the positive terminal of the cell.
- CHRG and STDBY: open-drain outputs for the charging and standby LEDs.

The datasheet gives the charge current $I_{\text{BAT}}$ in amperes from the resistor $R_{\text{PROG}}$ in ohms:

$$I_{\text{BAT}} = \frac{1200}{R_{\text{PROG}}}$$

A smaller resistor gives a larger current. The maker's table of measured values differs by a few percent:

| PROG Resistor | By the formula | Maker's table |
|---------------|---------------|---------------|
| 10 kΩ | 120 mA | 130 mA |
| 5 kΩ | 240 mA | 250 mA |
| 2.4 kΩ | 500 mA | not listed |
| 2 kΩ | 600 mA | 580 mA |
| 1.2 kΩ | 1000 mA | 1000 mA |

The cell decides the value. Most small cells allow a charge current of 0.5 to 1 times their capacity: 250 to 500 mA for a 500 mAh cell. This board uses 2.4 kΩ, which gives 1200 / 2400 = 0.5 A, the upper limit for that cell. A smaller cell needs a larger resistor, and so does a small solar panel (10 kΩ for about 100 mA).

### Solar and USB Input: Diode-OR

Two sources feed one charger, and neither may push current into the other. A diode passes current in one direction only, like a one-way valve on each of two pipes that join, so each source gets its own Schottky diode (SS14, which loses only about 0.3V) to the TP4056 VIN pin. This is a diode-OR: the source with the higher voltage supplies the current.

- USB VBUS connects through D2 to TP4056 VIN.
- The solar panel connects through D1 to TP4056 VIN.
- A 10 µF capacitor on VIN smooths the input.

When both are present, USB usually supplies the current: the voltage of a small panel falls under load, and USB holds 5V.

### Battery Protection: DW01A + 8205A

The charger protects the cell only while it charges. The protection circuit watches the cell all the time and disconnects it in three cases:

| Protection | Threshold | Action | What it prevents |
|-----------|-----------|--------|------------------|
| Overcharge | ~4.3V | Disconnects charge path | Overheating and fire, if the charger fails |
| Overdischarge | ~2.4V | Disconnects load | A cell emptied so far that it is damaged for good |
| Overcurrent | ~3A | Disconnects load | Heat and fire from a short circuit |

The DW01A measures the cell: its supply pins connect between B+ and B-, the two terminals of the cell. The 8205A is a pair of N-channel MOSFETs, used as two switches in series in the negative line, between B- and system ground. The DW01A's OD pin (discharge control) drives one gate and its OC pin (charge control) drives the other. On a fault it switches one MOSFET off, and the cell is disconnected.

This circuit does not protect against a cell connected backwards: check the polarity with a meter.

### Voltage Regulation: ME6211 LDO

The ME6211C33M5G makes a steady 3.3V from the cell voltage (3.0V to 4.2V). It is chosen for its quiescent current of about 40 µA: a common regulator such as the AMS1117 takes about 5 mA for itself, a hundred times more, and would empty the cell in a few days. For more on choosing a regulator, see [Analog Electronics: Power Supply Design](/education/analog-electronics/power-supply-design/).

- Output: 3.3V, up to 500 mA
- Dropout voltage: ~100 mV at light loads
- Capacitors: 10 µF ceramic at the input, 10 µF and 100 nF ceramic at the output

:::caution[LDO Dropout]
A regulator needs its input a little above its output. Below about 3.4V at the cell, the LDO output follows the cell downward. The ESP32-C3 still works down to about 3.0V, but WiFi becomes unreliable below about 3.1V. The DW01A disconnects the cell at ~2.4V.
:::

### Power Budget Calculation

A power budget is a list of every current the cell must supply. Start with the parts that stay powered during deep sleep:

| Component | Sleep Current | Notes |
|-----------|--------------|-------|
| ESP32-C3 deep sleep | ~5 µA | RTC memory retained |
| BME280 sleep mode | ~0.1 µA | Powered off via MOSFET: 0 µA |
| ME6211 quiescent | ~40 µA | Always on |
| TP4056 standby | ~2 µA | Battery full, no input |
| DW01A + 8205A | ~3 µA | Protection circuit |
| Pull-up/pull-down resistors | ~1 µA | Depends on values chosen |
| **Total sleep current** | **~51 µA** | Conservative estimate |

The node takes a large current for a short time and a small one for a long time, and the cell sees the average: the sleep current plus the wake current times the fraction of time awake. Battery life in hours is the capacity in mAh divided by the average current in mA.

**Battery life estimate with a 500 mAh cell:**

- Sleep current: ~51 µA
- Wake current: ~80 mA while the WiFi radio transmits
- Wake duration: ~5 seconds every 10 minutes, a fraction of 5 / 600 = 0.0083
- Average current: $51\,\mu\text{A} + (80\,\text{mA} \times 0.0083) \approx 51\,\mu\text{A} + 664\,\mu\text{A} \approx 715\,\mu\text{A}$
- Battery life: $500\,\text{mAh} / 0.715\,\text{mA} \approx 699\,\text{hours} \approx$ **29 days with WiFi**

The 5 seconds awake use more than nine tenths of the energy. For BLE-only transmission (peak ~15 mA, wake ~2 seconds, a fraction of 2 / 600 = 0.0033):

- Average current: $51\,\mu\text{A} + (15\,\text{mA} \times 0.0033) \approx 51\,\mu\text{A} + 50\,\mu\text{A} \approx 101\,\mu\text{A}$
- Battery life: $500\,\text{mAh} / 0.101\,\text{mA} \approx 4950\,\text{hours} \approx$ **206 days**

These are upper estimates: a cell also loses a few percent of its charge each month by itself. A small solar panel can keep the node running without a visit. The wake currents and times are assumptions, and the BLE figure is optimistic (the processor alone takes some milliamperes awake): measure them on your board, as the check at the end shows.

A node in a field should also report its cell voltage, so you see a cell failing weeks before the node stops. Two 1 MΩ resistors from B+ to GND, the middle to an ADC pin (GPIO1) with 100 nF to GND, give half the cell voltage (2.1 V at 4.2 V) for 2.1 µA.

## Deep Sleep Considerations for PCB Design

<InArticleAd />


Firmware puts the chip to sleep, but pull-up resistors, LEDs and parts that stay powered can still waste current. With BLE, half of the average current is sleep current, so every microampere counts.

### Resistor Values Matter

A pull-up resistor holds a pin high. When the pin is held low, current flows from the 3.3V rail through the resistor to ground, all day. The current is 3.3V divided by the resistance:

| Resistor Value | Leak Current |
|---------------|-------------|
| 4.7 kΩ | 702 µA |
| 10 kΩ | 330 µA |
| 47 kΩ | 70 µA |
| 100 kΩ | 33 µA |

One 10 kΩ resistor that leaks all day takes six times the sleep current of the whole board. So:

- Use 100 kΩ or higher for pull-ups and pull-downs where possible
- For the I2C pull-ups, use 10 kΩ in place of the common 4.7 kΩ. The I2C lines rest high, so these resistors leak only while data moves. At a 100 kHz clock, 10 kΩ works for a bus shorter than 10 cm
- The EN pin pull-up on the ESP32-C3 can be 100 kΩ with a 1 µF capacitor

### LED Power Waste

An indicator LED takes 5 to 20 mA: a power LED left on during deep sleep takes a hundred times more than the rest of the board. You can:

- Leave the power LED off the board (the simplest choice for a node in the field)
- Add a solder jumper in series with the LED, and open it after testing
- Switch the LED with a GPIO, so it lights only when the processor is awake

On this board the CHRG and STDBY LEDs are fed from the charger input, so they take nothing from the cell.

### Switchable Sensor Power

The BME280 takes about 0.1 µA in its sleep mode, but other sensors take much more when idle. A P-channel MOSFET in the sensor's supply line, controlled by a GPIO, removes that current and gives the sensor a clean start each time.

The circuit uses a SI2301 P-MOSFET:

- Source: 3.3V rail
- Drain: sensor supply (BME280 VDD)
- Gate: GPIO pin, with a 100 kΩ pull-up to 3.3V

A P-channel MOSFET switches on when its gate is below its source. When the GPIO drives LOW, the MOSFET is on and the sensor has power. When the GPIO is HIGH, or floating during deep sleep (the pull-up then holds the gate high), the MOSFET is off and the sensor takes nothing.

### USB-UART Bridge Current

Many ESP32 boards carry a separate USB-to-UART chip (CP2102 or CH340) for programming, which takes 5 to 15 mA even when idle. The ESP32-C3 has USB built into the chip, so this board needs no such part.

### USB-C Connector

<Steps>

1. **Place the USB-C connector (16-pin, USB 2.0)**

   For power and programming you need six signals: VBUS, GND, D+, D-, CC1, and CC2. Use a 16-pin USB 2.0 receptacle, because the 6-pin USB-C parts carry power only and have no data pins. Place two 5.1 kΩ resistors from CC1 and CC2 to GND. A USB-C source gives no power until it sees this resistance: 5.1 kΩ is the value that means "a device is connected, supply 5V".

   Connect VBUS through Schottky diode D2 to the TP4056 VIN net.

2. **Connect the USB data lines**

   The ESP32-C3 native USB pins are GPIO18 (D-) and GPIO19 (D+). Route these directly from the USB-C connector to the module pins. No external components are needed; the ESP32-C3 handles USB internally.

</Steps>

### TP4056 Charging Circuit

<Steps>

1. **Place the TP4056 IC**

   Connect VIN to the diode-OR output from USB and solar. Place a 10 µF input capacitor close to the VIN pin.

2. **Set the charge current**

   Place a 2.4 kΩ resistor from the PROG pin to GND, for 1200 / 2400 = 0.5 A, the limit of a 500 mAh cell. If a small solar panel is the main source, 10 kΩ (120 mA by the formula) matches what the panel can give.

3. **Add status LEDs**

   Connect CHRG to LED1 (red) through a 1 kΩ resistor to VIN. Connect STDBY to LED2 (green) through a 1 kΩ resistor to VIN. Both pins are open-drain: they switch the LED current to ground.

4. **Connect BAT to the protection circuit**

   The TP4056 BAT pin connects to the B+ net, the positive terminal of the cell.

</Steps>

### Solar Panel Input

<Steps>

1. **Place the solar panel connector (J3)**

   Use a JST-PH 2-pin connector. Connect the positive pin through Schottky diode D1 to the TP4056 VIN net (same net as the USB diode output).

2. **Check the diode direction**

   D1 has its anode on the panel and its cathode on VIN. It stops current from flowing back into the panel when USB is connected or when the panel is dark.

</Steps>

### Battery Protection Circuit

System ground (GND) and B- are separate nets. The two MOSFETs sit between them, so the board reaches the cell's negative terminal only through the protection.

<Steps>

1. **Place the DW01A**

   Connect VCC to B+ (battery positive). Connect GND (VSS) to B- (battery negative). The OD (discharge control) and OC (charge control) pins connect to the gates of the two MOSFETs in the 8205A.

2. **Place the 8205A dual MOSFET**

   The 8205A contains two N-channel MOSFETs in series, with their drains joined inside the package. Connect the source of one MOSFET to the battery negative terminal (B-). Connect the source of the other MOSFET to system ground (GND). The gates connect to the DW01A OD pin (the MOSFET on the B- side) and OC pin (the MOSFET on the GND side).

3. **Add the current sense resistor path**

   Place a 1 kΩ resistor (R8) from the DW01A CS pin to system ground (GND), which is the far side of the two MOSFETs from the battery. The CS pin measures the voltage across both MOSFETs. That voltage rises with the load current, so it shows an overcurrent.

</Steps>

### Battery Connector

Place a JST-PH 2-pin connector (J2) for the battery. Connect the positive pin to B+ and the negative pin to B- (not to GND). Mark the polarity beside the connector on the silkscreen, so a cell can be checked before it is plugged in.

### ME6211 LDO

<Steps>

1. **Place the ME6211C33M5G**

   Connect VIN to the B+ net (the same net as the TP4056 BAT pin) and the ground pin to system GND, so the load current returns through the protection MOSFETs. Place a 10 µF input capacitor close to VIN.

2. **Add output capacitors**

   Place a 10 µF and a 100 nF ceramic on the 3.3V output, close to the VOUT pin. The ME6211 needs at least 1 µF there to stay stable.

3. **Connect EN**

   Tie the enable pin to VIN, so the regulator is on whenever the cell is connected.

</Steps>

### ESP32-C3-MINI-1 Module

<Steps>

1. **Place the module**

   The module has its pads on the underside (no castellations) and a large ground pad in the centre. Connect 3V3 (pin 3) to the 3.3V rail, and all GND pins and the centre pad to GND.

2. **Add decoupling**

   Place a 10 µF and a 100 nF capacitor close to the 3V3 pin. They supply the bursts of current the radio takes.

3. **EN (enable) circuit**

   Connect a 100 kΩ pull-up from EN to 3.3V and a 1 µF capacitor from EN to GND. Together they hold EN low for a moment at power-on, until the supply is steady. Connect the reset button (SW1) between EN and GND.

4. **Boot button**

   Connect SW2 between GPIO9 and GND. GPIO9 held low during reset puts the ESP32-C3 into download mode, to load firmware over USB.

5. **I2C pins for BME280**

   Use GPIO4 as SDA and GPIO5 as SCL, each with a 10 kΩ pull-up to the switched sensor rail (the drain of Q2), not to the main 3.3V rail.

6. **Sensor power control**

   Connect GPIO3 to the gate of the P-MOSFET (Q2) through a 100 Ω resistor, which softens the switching edge.

</Steps>

### BME280 Sensor

<Steps>

1. **Place the BME280**

   The BME280 is in a small LGA-8 package (2.5 x 2.5 mm). Connect VDD to the switched 3.3V rail (drain of Q2) and GND to ground, with a 100 nF capacitor close to VDD.

2. **Set the I2C address**

   Connect SDO to GND for I2C address 0x76 (the usual choice), or to VDD for 0x77.

3. **Connect I2C**

   Connect SDA to GPIO4 and SCL to GPIO5 on the ESP32-C3. The pull-ups go on the switched rail, with the sensor. On the main rail they would hold the lines at 3.3 V while the sensor has no supply, and current would flow through the sensor's input protection diodes into its empty supply: about (3.3 − 0.6) V / 10 kΩ ≈ 0.27 mA a line, ten times the sleep budget of the whole board.

4. **CSB pin**

   Connect CSB to VDD (the switched rail) to select I2C mode. If CSB is left floating, the BME280 may enter SPI mode.

</Steps>

### Run ERC and Assign Footprints

<Steps>

1. **Run Electrical Rules Check**

   Correct unconnected pins and conflicting outputs. Add power flags on the battery and USB VBUS nets if KiCad reports them as undriven.

2. **Assign footprints**

   Give each symbol its footprint (the copper pattern of its package). The ESP32-C3-MINI-1 footprint may have to come from Espressif's KiCad library.

3. **Generate the netlist**

   Export the netlist and open the PCB editor.

</Steps>

## PCB Layout

<InArticleAd />


Three things decide this layout: the antenna must be clear of copper, the charger must lose its heat, and the temperature sensor must stay away from that heat. The board is 45 x 30 mm, with 2 layers.

```text
  ┌──────────────────────────────┬──────────┐
  │ ┌───────┐ ┌────────┐ ┌───────┤ antenna  │
  │ │ USB-C │ │ TP4056 │ │ESP32  │ KEEP-OUT │
  │ └───────┘ │ (warm) │ │C3     │ no copper│
  │           └────────┘ │module │          │
  │ ┌────────────┐ ┌───┐ └───────┤          │
  │ │ protection │ │LDO│         └──────────┤
  │ └────────────┘ └───┘       ┌────────┐   │
  │ [battery J2] [solar J3]    │ BME280 │   │
  └────────────────────────────┴────────┴───┘
```

### Board Setup

<Steps>

1. **Set the board outline**

   Draw a 45 x 30 mm rectangle on the Edge.Cuts layer. Round the corners with a 1 mm radius, so they do not catch.

2. **Configure design rules**

   Set the minimum trace width and clearance to 0.15 mm, which the factory can make. Use 0.4 mm or wider for power traces (VBAT, 3.3V, GND) and 0.25 mm for USB data.

3. **Set up the ground pour**

   Plan copper fills connected to GND on both layers. Pour them after all traces are routed.

</Steps>

### Component Placement

<Steps>

1. **Place the ESP32-C3-MINI-1 module**

   Put it at one short edge of the board, with the antenna flush with the edge or past it, and no copper on any layer within 2 mm of the antenna.

2. **Place the USB-C connector**

   Put it on the opposite short edge, so the USB cable is far from the antenna.

3. **Place the TP4056 and protection circuit**

   Group the TP4056, DW01A, 8205A and their small parts near the USB-C connector and battery connector. The TP4056 becomes warm while it charges, so give it a copper pour to spread the heat.

4. **Place the battery and solar connectors**

   Position J2 (battery) and J3 (solar) along one long edge of the board. Through-hole JST connectors need clearance for the housing.

5. **Place the ME6211 LDO**

   Position it between the charging/protection section and the ESP32-C3. Keep input and output capacitors within 3 mm of the IC.

6. **Place the BME280**

   Put it near the board edge, away from the TP4056 and the LDO. A sensor beside a warm charger reports the temperature of the charger, not of the air. A slot cut in the board between them reduces the heat that reaches it.

7. **Place buttons and LEDs**

   Put the reset and boot buttons where a finger can reach them, and the charging LEDs near the USB connector.

</Steps>

### Antenna Keepout Zone

:::caution[Antenna Clearance]
The antenna is a copper pattern at one end of the module. Copper near it changes the frequency it is tuned to, and the WiFi and BLE range falls. Keep all layers free of copper, traces and parts around it.
:::

In KiCad, draw a keepout area on both copper layers over the antenna region, set to prohibit copper fills and tracks. The Espressif datasheet gives the dimensions, and the [ESP32 devkit lesson](/education/pcb-design-kicad/esp32-wifi-bluetooth-devkit/) explains the antenna rules in full.

### Thermal Management for TP4056

The TP4056 is a linear charger: the voltage it does not pass to the cell becomes heat inside it. Its SOP-8 package has a metal pad underneath (ESOP-8) that passes the heat to the board. The heat is the voltage across the charger multiplied by the current. At 500 mA from 5V into a cell at 3.7V:

$$P = (5\,\text{V} - 3.7\,\text{V}) \times 0.5\,\text{A} = 0.65\,\text{W}$$

Into a deeply discharged cell at 3.0V it is about 1 W. After D2, VIN is nearer 4.7 V, which lowers these to about 0.5 W and 0.85 W. The TP4056 reduces its current by itself when its die reaches about 120 °C, so a charger short of copper charges slowly rather than failing. Make the copper around the pad and GND pins as large as you can, and join it to a pour on both layers with several vias.

### Routing Guidelines

<Steps>

1. **Route power traces first**

   Start with VBAT, 3.3V, and GND, 0.4 mm wide or more. Keep the path from the LDO output to the ESP32-C3 supply pin short.

2. **Route USB data lines**

   Keep D+ and D- side by side at a constant spacing, with lengths equal to within 0.5 mm, and away from the antenna.

3. **Route I2C lines**

   Route SDA and SCL to the BME280. They are slow signals. Keep them short and away from the antenna area.

4. **Fill copper zones**

   Pour ground on both layers. Join the two pours with vias, most of all near the module's ground pad and the TP4056.

5. **Run DRC**

   The design rules check finds clearance errors, unrouted nets and traces that are too narrow. Correct them before you make the Gerber files.

</Steps>

:::tip[Instrument it]
This board turns a breadboard sensor into a node you can leave in a field. Give the sensor a vent, so it reads the real air and water stays out. Keep the antenna clear of the ground pour and of the battery. Choose the cell for the reporting rate you really need. The node can then [report from anywhere](/education/iot-systems/) over WiFi, cellular, or LoRaWAN to a [dashboard](https://siliconwit.io).
:::

## Manufacturing

<InArticleAd />


A factory works from Gerber files (one drawing for each layer) and a drill file. Export them from KiCad with File, then Plot. Select all copper layers (F.Cu, B.Cu), silkscreen layers, mask layers, and Edge.Cuts. Generate the drill file in Excellon format. Compress all files into a single ZIP.

Upload to JLCPCB or another fabricator. For this 2-layer board the standard settings are enough:

| Parameter | Setting |
|-----------|---------|
| Layers | 2 |
| PCB thickness | 1.6 mm |
| Surface finish | HASL (lead-free) or ENIG |
| Copper weight | 1 oz |
| Solder mask | Green (or your preference) |
| Min trace/space | 6/6 mil (0.15/0.15 mm) |

Order a stencil for the top layer. The BME280 (LGA-8) and the ESP32-C3 module have their pads underneath, so they are soldered with paste and hot air or a reflow oven.

Before you order, do the six checks of the [breakout board](/education/pcb-design-kicad/atmega328p-breakout-board-through-hole/#check-before-you-order), and on this board three more: the PROG resistor value in the BOM is 2.4 kΩ (not the 1.2 kΩ of the downloadable PDF), the battery connector's pin 1 matches the cells you will use, and the antenna area is empty on both layers in the Gerber viewer. A solar panel's open-circuit voltage must stay below the TP4056's absolute maximum input (8 V in its data sheet); a "5 V" panel can give 6 to 7 V with no load.

Before the first charge, check the cell's polarity with a meter, and stay with the board while it charges.

## Firmware: Deep Sleep Sensor Reading

<InArticleAd />


The firmware follows the pattern of every battery sensor: wake, read, send, sleep. This example uses the Arduino framework for the ESP32-C3. It reads the BME280 over I2C, prints the data to USB serial, and enters deep sleep for 10 minutes.

### Arduino Setup

Install the ESP32 board package in Arduino IDE (or PlatformIO). Select "ESP32C3 Dev Module" as the board, and set Tools > USB CDC On Boot to "Enabled" so that `Serial` uses the native USB port. Install the Adafruit BME280 library and the Adafruit Unified Sensor library from the Library Manager.

### Complete Sketch

```cpp
#include <Wire.h>
#include <Adafruit_Sensor.h>
#include <Adafruit_BME280.h>

// Pin definitions
#define SDA_PIN 4
#define SCL_PIN 5
#define SENSOR_POWER_PIN 3

// Deep sleep duration: 10 minutes in microseconds
#define SLEEP_DURATION_US 600000000ULL

// BME280 I2C address (SDO to GND = 0x76)
#define BME280_ADDR 0x76

Adafruit_BME280 bme;

void goToSleep();  // defined below

void setup() {
  // Initialize USB serial
  Serial.begin(115200);
  delay(100);

  // Power on the BME280 sensor
  pinMode(SENSOR_POWER_PIN, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(SENSOR_POWER_PIN, LOW); // P-MOSFET: LOW = ON

  // Wait for sensor power to stabilize
  delay(50);

  // Initialize I2C
  Wire.begin(SDA_PIN, SCL_PIN);

  // Initialize BME280
  if (!bme.begin(BME280_ADDR, &Wire)) {
    Serial.println("BME280 not found. Check wiring.");
    goToSleep();
    return;
  }

  // Set BME280 to forced mode for single reading
  bme.setSampling(
    Adafruit_BME280::MODE_FORCED,
    Adafruit_BME280::SAMPLING_X1,  // temperature
    Adafruit_BME280::SAMPLING_X1,  // pressure
    Adafruit_BME280::SAMPLING_X1,  // humidity
    Adafruit_BME280::FILTER_OFF,
    Adafruit_BME280::STANDBY_MS_0_5
  );

  // Take a forced measurement
  bme.takeForcedMeasurement();

  // Read sensor data
  float temperature = bme.readTemperature();
  float humidity = bme.readHumidity();
  float pressure = bme.readPressure() / 100.0F; // hPa

  // Print data
  Serial.println("=== IoT Sensor Reading ===");
  Serial.print("Temperature: ");
  Serial.print(temperature, 1);
  Serial.println(" C");
  Serial.print("Humidity: ");
  Serial.print(humidity, 1);
  Serial.println(" %");
  Serial.print("Pressure: ");
  Serial.print(pressure, 1);
  Serial.println(" hPa");
  Serial.println("==========================");
  Serial.flush();

  // Power off the sensor
  digitalWrite(SENSOR_POWER_PIN, HIGH); // P-MOSFET: HIGH = OFF

  // Go to deep sleep
  goToSleep();
}

void goToSleep() {
  Serial.println("Entering deep sleep for 10 minutes...");
  Serial.flush();

  // Configure deep sleep timer
  esp_sleep_enable_timer_wakeup(SLEEP_DURATION_US);

  // Enter deep sleep
  esp_deep_sleep_start();
}

void loop() {
  // This never executes. After deep sleep,
  // the ESP32-C3 resets and runs setup() again.
}
```

### How It Works

Deep sleep on the ESP32-C3 ends with a reset. The program starts again from `setup()` each time, not from where it stopped, which is why `loop()` is empty. A small memory (the RTC memory) keeps its contents through sleep, for a boot counter or stored readings.

The order of events:

1. Wake from deep sleep (or first power-on)
2. Drive GPIO3 LOW to switch on the sensor power MOSFET, and wait 50 ms
3. Start I2C and the BME280
4. Take one forced measurement (a single reading, the mode that uses least power)
5. Print temperature, humidity and pressure over USB serial
6. Drive GPIO3 HIGH to switch the sensor off
7. Enter deep sleep for 10 minutes

To send the data over WiFi, connect and send (an HTTP POST or an MQTT publish) between steps 5 and 6. WiFi adds about 3 to 5 seconds of wake time at 80 to 150 mA.

### Check it

The promise of this board is months on one cell. Measure what the budget rests on:

- **Charge current.** At full current the PROG pin reads 1.0 V; the current is (V_PROG / R_PROG) × 1200, so 0.5 A on 2.4 kΩ. Lower on a warm charger means the thermal limit is acting.
- **Sleep current.** USB and panel unplugged, a meter on its µA range in series with the cell, node asleep: about 50 µA. Milliamperes mean a part still awake: an LED, a pull-up pulled low, a back-powered sensor.
- **The measuring trap.** On its µA range a meter puts about 100 Ω in series; at the 80 mA of a wake that drops 8 V and the node resets. Short the meter during the wake, or use a current-profiling tool.
- **Wake energy.** Time one wake (serial log, or an oscilloscope across 1 Ω in the supply), put the measured time and current in the budget, and recompute the days.
- **Temperature.** Compare with a thermometer beside the board, charger idle and then charging. A difference only while charging is heat from the TP4056.
