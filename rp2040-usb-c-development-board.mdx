The RP2040 is a fast microcontroller (MCU) that costs about 1 USD, but the chip alone cannot run a program. It has no program memory inside, so a separate flash memory chip must sit beside it. Its 56 contacts are hidden under the package, where a soldering iron cannot reach. It needs two supply voltages, a crystal and a few resistors of exact value, and a board with one of these wrong is silent when you plug it in. In this lesson you design a complete RP2040 board with USB-C on four copper layers, solder it with paste and hot air, and load a first program without a programmer. #KiCad #RP2040 #PIO

The chip needs 3.3V for its pins and 1.1V for its processor core. A regulator on the board makes the 3.3V, and a regulator inside the RP2040 makes the 1.1V from it:

```text
  USB-C 5V ──► NCP1117 ──► 3.3V
                             │
              ┌──────────────┴───┐
              ▼                  ▼
        ┌───────────┐     ┌────────────┐
        │  RP2040   │     │  W25Q128   │
        │ pins 3.3V │     │ flash chip │
        │           │     └────────────┘
        │ internal  │
        │ 1.1V reg. ├──► DVDD (core)
        └───────────┘
        1 µF at its input, 1 µF at its output
```

## RP2040 Architecture Overview

<InArticleAd />


Three properties of the RP2040 decide what the board must contain.

It has no flash memory inside. The program lives in the external flash chip, and the RP2040 runs it directly from there (execute in place, XIP), so the six QSPI tracks carry every instruction and must be routed with care. The chip does hold a small permanent program, the boot ROM, that nobody can erase. Hold the BOOTSEL button while you plug in USB, and the boot ROM makes the board appear on the computer as a drive. You copy a `.uf2` program file to that drive, and the board is programmed. No programmer is needed, and no faulty program can stop this from working.

It has USB inside. The USB transceiver (the PHY) is part of the chip, so D+ and D- go from the connector to the chip through two resistors and nothing else. USB needs an exact 48 MHz clock. A 12 MHz crystal gives the reference, and a PLL inside the chip (a circuit that multiplies a clock to a higher frequency) makes 48 MHz from it.

It has two ARM Cortex-M0+ cores at up to 133 MHz, 264 KB of RAM, and Programmable I/O (PIO). PIO is eight small state machines that each run a tiny program to make or read signals with exact timing, without using the processor. It drives the RGB LED on this board.

The chip also has 30 GPIO pins, a 4-channel 12-bit converter for analog voltages (ADC), and two each of SPI, I2C and UART.

## QFN Package Considerations

<InArticleAd />


The RP2040 comes in a QFN-56 package, a 7x7 mm square. QFN means Quad Flat No-lead: the package has no legs. Its 56 contacts are flat pads under its four edges, 0.4 mm from one centre to the next. The package is small and its short connections suit fast signals, but an ordinary soldering iron cannot reach the pads.

### QFN Soldering Requirements

In the middle of the underside is one large pad, the exposed pad. It is the chip's only ground connection, and it carries the chip's heat into the board. It must be soldered to the ground plane, or the chip does not work.

You solder a QFN by reflow: you put solder paste on the pads, place the parts in the paste, and heat the whole board until the paste melts.

<Steps>

1. Solder paste stencil. Order a stainless steel stencil with your boards. It is a thin sheet with an opening over every pad. You draw solder paste across it with a blade, and paste stays only on the pads. Over the exposed pad, the opening is divided into a grid of small rectangles (3x3 or 4x4). One large opening would put too much paste there, and the chip would float on it with its edge pads in the air.

2. Reflow soldering. Heat the board with a hot air station, a small reflow oven, or a toaster oven with a temperature controller. The board warms to about 150 C first, then reaches 245 to 260 C for lead-free solder.

3. Placement accuracy. Use fine tweezers and a magnifier. Turn the chip so its pin 1 dot is at the pin 1 mark printed on the board. Melted solder pulls a chip into line by surface tension, but only if you place it within about 0.1 mm.

</Steps>

### PCB Pad Design for QFN

Check three things in the KiCad footprint for the RP2040:

- Edge pads: each pad is as wide as the contact (about 0.2 mm) and extends 0.2 mm outside the package edge, so you can see the solder joint from above.
- Exposed pad: the copper pad matches the size in the data sheet (about 3.2 x 3.2 mm), and its paste layer is a grid.
- Thermal vias: a 3x3 or 4x4 grid of small vias (0.3 mm drill, 0.6 mm pad) in the exposed pad joins it to the inner ground plane and carries heat into it. Cover or fill the vias on the bottom side, so solder does not run down through them.

<InArticleAd />


Build the schematic one small circuit at a time, in the order below.

The PDF is an earlier drawing. It has no polyfuse and no WS2812B (it has a power LED and a plain LED on GPIO25), and it puts 100 µF on each side of the regulator, where USB allows at most 10 µF on VBUS. It also uses a 4 MB flash chip, a 1 kΩ resistor in series with the crystal and another in series with the reset button, a 4-pin debug header, and other part numbers (J3 is the USB connector, SW1 is reset and SW2 is BOOTSEL). Where the two differ, follow the text.

### Power Supply Design

The RP2040 has two supplies:

- IOVDD (3.3V) feeds the GPIO pins. It has six pins. A fast chip takes its current in short pulses, so each pin gets its own 100 nF decoupling capacitor within 2 mm, which delivers those pulses.
- DVDD (1.1V) feeds the processor cores and memory. A regulator inside the RP2040 makes it: 3.3V goes in at VREG_VIN and 1.1V comes out at VREG_VOUT. This regulator is linear. It needs a 1 µF capacitor at its input and a 1 µF capacitor at its output, and no inductor.

The power path is: USB VBUS (5V), polyfuse F1, NCP1117-3.3 regulator, 3.3V. A polyfuse is a fuse that resets itself, and it limits the current if the board has a short.

**LDO circuit (NCP1117-3.3):**
- Input: USB VBUS (5V) through polyfuse F1
- Input capacitor: 10 µF ceramic on VIN
- Output capacitor: 10 µF ceramic on VOUT, which the regulator needs to stay stable
- Output: 3.3V to IOVDD, VREG_VIN, flash VCC, WS2812B VDD

**Core regulator external components:**
- VREG_VIN: connect to 3.3V, with 1 µF to GND
- VREG_VOUT: connect directly to both DVDD pins, with a 1 µF capacitor close to VREG_VOUT and 100 nF at the DVDD pins
- No inductor: the RP2040 core regulator is linear (the later RP2350 is the chip that needs one)

### USB-C Connection

The RP2040 connects to USB directly, with no USB bridge chip. Use a 16-pin USB-C receptacle: a 6-pin one carries power only and has no data pins.

| USB-C Pin | Connection |
|-----------|------------|
| VBUS | 5V rail through polyfuse F1 |
| CC1 | 5.1 kΩ to GND (identifies as UFP/device) |
| CC2 | 5.1 kΩ to GND |
| D+ | 27 Ω series resistor to RP2040 USB_DP (pin 47) |
| D- | 27 Ω series resistor to RP2040 USB_DM (pin 46) |
| GND | Board ground |

A USB-C host (a computer, hub or charger) gives no 5V until it sees a device. One 5.1 kΩ resistor from each CC pin to GND is that sign, and the USB-C standard fixes the value. The RP2040's USB driver has a low output resistance, and 27 Ω in series with each data line raises it to what the cable expects. The RP2040 data sheet asks for this value.

### QSPI Flash Connection

The W25Q128JV connects to the RP2040's six QSPI pins. These pins are not GPIO: the chip reserves them for the flash memory it starts from.

| Flash Pin | RP2040 Pin | Function |
|-----------|------------|----------|
| CLK | QSPI_SCLK | Clock |
| CS# | QSPI_SS | Chip select (active low) |
| DI (IO0) | QSPI_SD0 | Data in / IO0 |
| DO (IO1) | QSPI_SD1 | Data out / IO1 |
| WP# (IO2) | QSPI_SD2 | Write protect / IO2 |
| HOLD# (IO3) | QSPI_SD3 | Hold / IO3 |
| VCC | 3.3V | Power (100 nF + 4.7 µF decoupling) |
| GND | GND | Ground |

Add a 1 kΩ pull-up resistor from CS# to 3.3V. While the power rises, the RP2040 does not yet drive this line, and the resistor keeps the flash chip unselected.

### Crystal Oscillator

The 12 MHz crystal connects between XIN (pin 20) and XOUT (pin 21), with a 15 pF load capacitor from each pin to ground.

A crystal runs at its marked frequency only when it sees its specified load capacitance $C_L$. The two capacitors act in series, and the pins and tracks add about 5 pF of stray capacitance, so each capacitor should be $2\,(C_L - 5\,\text{pF})$. For a crystal with $C_L$ = 15 pF this gives 20 pF. With the 15 pF fitted here, the crystal sees $15/2 + 5 = 12.5\,\text{pF}$ and runs a few tens of parts per million fast. Full-speed USB allows 2,500 parts per million, so the board works. Fit 20 pF capacitors, or a crystal with a load capacitance of 12 pF, if you need the exact frequency.

In the layout:

- Place the crystal within 5 mm of the RP2040
- Surround its tracks with a ground pour, which shields them
- Route no track between the two crystal pads

### BOOTSEL and Reset

**BOOTSEL button:** The button connects QSPI_SS, the chip select line of the flash, to GND. If QSPI_SS is low when the chip starts, the boot ROM does not start the program in flash. It shows the board to the computer as a drive and waits for a `.uf2` file. After the start, the RP2040 drives QSPI_SS as an output and does not read the button.

**Reset button:** RUN is the reset pin, and the chip restarts when it is pulled to 0 V. Connect a 10 kΩ pull-up from RUN to 3.3V and a tactile switch from RUN to GND.

To enter this drive mode on a powered board: hold BOOTSEL, press and release reset, then release BOOTSEL.

### SWD Debug Header

SWD (Serial Wire Debug) is the two-wire connection through which a debugger loads a program, stops it and reads its variables. The RP2040 has it on fixed pins:
- SWCLK (pin 24)
- SWDIO (pin 25)
- GND

A 1x3 pin header is enough. Some debug probes (Raspberry Pi Debug Probe, Picoprobe) also use a serial port, so you may add a second header for UART0 TX and RX.

### WS2812B RGB LED

The WS2812B is an RGB LED with its own driver chip inside. One data line sets its colour:

- VDD: 3.3V (the data sheet range is 3.5 to 5.3V; most units work at 3.3V; you can also power it from VBUS at 5V)
- DIN: connect to a GPIO (for example, GPIO16) through a 330 Ω series resistor, which softens the signal edge
- DOUT: leave unconnected (it would feed the next LED of a chain)
- GND: board ground
- Place a 100 nF decoupling capacitor between VDD and GND, close to the LED

### GPIO Headers and PIO Pin Labeling

Bring 26 GPIO pins to two 1x20 headers, in the order of the Raspberry Pi Pico where you can. The headers also carry 3.3V, GND, VBUS, and ADC_VREF.

PIO can use any GPIO, so every GPIO pin on the headers is a PIO pin. In the printed text on the board (the silkscreen), mark the pins that can measure a voltage (GPIO26 to GPIO29) with "ADC", and mark the default SPI, I2C and UART pins.

### Electrical Rules Check

The electrical rules check (ERC) finds pins left open and nets that nothing drives. Run it when the schematic is complete:

<Steps>

1. Check power flags. Every power net (3.3V, 1.1V, VBUS, GND) needs a power flag symbol or a regulator output that drives it. VREG_VOUT drives the 1.1V net.

2. Check the pins that must not be left open. TESTEN (pin 19) goes to GND. USB_VDD (pin 48) goes to 3.3V with a 100 nF decoupling capacitor. All six IOVDD pins and ADC_AVDD (pin 43) go to 3.3V.

3. Check the decoupling. Each IOVDD pin and each DVDD pin has its 100 nF capacitor in the schematic.

4. Assign footprints. Open the footprint assignment tool and check that every part has its footprint, the pattern of pads it is soldered to. Check the RP2040 QFN-56 (7x7mm, 0.4mm pitch) and the W25Q128JV SOIC-8 (the wide, 208 mil version) with most care.

</Steps>

## PCB Layout: 4-Layer Design

<InArticleAd />


### Layer Stackup

Use the stack-up (the order of copper layers and insulation) of the [STM32 USB-C four-layer board](/education/pcb-design-kicad/stm32-usb-c-four-layer-pcb/):

| Layer | Function | Copper Weight |
|-------|----------|---------------|
| F.Cu (Layer 1) | Signal routing, component pads | 1 oz |
| In1.Cu (Layer 2) | Ground plane (solid, unbroken) | 0.5 oz |
| In2.Cu (Layer 3) | 3.3V power plane | 0.5 oz |
| B.Cu (Layer 4) | Signal routing, bottom components | 1 oz |

A fast signal returns to its source through the ground plane, directly under its own track. With a whole plane on Layer 2, every track on Layer 1 has that path 0.2 mm below it. The 3.3V plane on Layer 3 reaches every part through a short via. The 1.1V core supply is a small area of copper near the RP2040, not a plane.

### QFN Pad Layout and Thermal Vias

Place the RP2040 near the center of the board. Turn it so that the QSPI pins face the flash chip and the USB pins face the edge with the USB-C connector, which keeps both sets of tracks short. Under the exposed pad, place the grid of thermal vias (3x3 or 4x4, 0.3 mm drill) from F.Cu to the In1.Cu ground plane. Give each via at least 0.15 mm of copper around its hole, a limit that standard factories can hold.

On the solder paste layer, check that the exposed pad is a grid of small rectangles with gaps between them (for example 3x3), for the reason given above: too much paste lifts the chip off its edge pads.

### QSPI Flash Routing

The flash clock can run at up to 133 MHz, the limit of the W25Q128JV. One clock period is then 7.5 ns, and the four data lines must all be read correctly inside it. A track that is longer delivers its bit later, and a long track with a via reflects part of the signal. A signal travels about 1 mm in 6 ps, so a 5 mm difference is only 30 ps: the rules below are easy to meet when the flash chip is close.

| Rule | Target |
|------|--------|
| Maximum track length (any QSPI signal) | 25 mm |
| Length matching (all 6 signals within) | 5 mm of each other |
| Routing layer | Single layer preferred (F.Cu), avoid vias |
| Track width | 0.15 to 0.2 mm (standard signal) |
| Spacing from other signals | At least 0.2 mm (2x track width) |
| Series termination (optional) | 33 Ω on CLK and IO0-IO3 |

<Steps>

1. **Place the flash chip close to the RP2040.** Put the W25Q128JV within 10 mm of the QSPI pins, on the same side of the board. Short tracks are also easy to make equal.

2. Route all six QSPI signals (CLK, CS, IO0 to IO3) on F.Cu, with no vias. A via adds about 0.5 nH of inductance and takes the signal away from its ground plane.

3. Make the lengths equal. Select the six QSPI nets in KiCad's length tuning tool, set a target (for example 15 mm), and add small bends to the shorter tracks until all six are within 5 mm of the longest. The data is read on the clock, so match the data tracks to CLK.

4. Add series resistors (optional). A 33 Ω resistor in series with CLK and each data line, within 3 mm of the RP2040 pins, absorbs reflections. Fit them if you run the flash at its top speed.

5. Keep ground around and under the tracks. Fill the space beside them on F.Cu with ground pour, and leave the In1.Cu plane whole under all six.

</Steps>

### USB Differential Pair Routing

USB sends each bit as the difference between D+ and D-, so the two tracks run side by side as one differential pair. The cable and the chip are made for 90 Ω between them, and the track width, the gap and the height above the ground plane set that value. The [STM32 USB-C four-layer board](/education/pcb-design-kicad/stm32-usb-c-four-layer-pcb/) explains how.

- Route D+ and D- as a differential pair on F.Cu, with no vias
- Keep the ground plane on In1.Cu whole beneath the pair
- Take the track width and gap for 90 Ω from the factory's impedance calculator for your stack-up. Start from 0.2 mm width and 0.15 mm gap on a standard 4-layer 1.6 mm board
- Make D+ and D- equal in length within 0.15 mm
- Keep the pair shorter than 50 mm
- Place the 27 Ω series resistors within 5 mm of the RP2040 pads

### Crystal Routing

A crystal track is a sensitive input: extra length adds stray capacitance and picks up noise.

- Keep the XIN and XOUT tracks shorter than 5 mm each, and 0.2 mm wide or more
- Route no other signal between the crystal pads
- Surround the crystal tracks with a ground pour on F.Cu
- Keep the ground plane on In1.Cu whole beneath the crystal

### Decoupling Capacitor Placement

Place a 100 nF 0402 capacitor within 2 mm of each of the six IOVDD pins and the two DVDD pins. Put the via from the capacitor's ground pad to the ground plane directly beside the pad. Every millimetre of track here slows the pulse of current the capacitor exists to deliver.

Place the core regulator's capacitors (1 µF on VREG_VIN, 100 nF + 1 µF on VREG_VOUT) within 5 mm of the VREG pins, and make the 1.1V track from VREG_VOUT to the two DVDD pins short and wide.

USB_VDD (pin 48) feeds the USB transceiver inside the chip. Place its 100 nF capacitor at the pin, with a short via to the ground plane.

### Via Stitching and Ground Pour

A ground pour on an outer layer helps only where vias join it to the ground plane. Finish the layout with:

- Ground pour on both F.Cu and B.Cu
- Stitching vias from the pours to In1.Cu every 3 to 5 mm around the board edge
- Ground vias on both sides of the USB pair, every 2 to 3 mm, and near the crystal
- No islands: connect or remove any piece of pour that no via reaches

## Assembly and Reflow

<InArticleAd />


Solder paste and hot plastic give off fumes, and the board reaches 260 C. Work with moving air, wear eye protection, and hold the hot board with tweezers.

<Steps>

1. Prepare the stencil jig. Tape three spare boards of the same thickness to a flat surface, so they form a pocket that holds your board without movement. Tape the stencil over it with its openings exactly on the pads.

2. Apply solder paste. Put a line of paste along one edge of the stencil. Draw it across with a metal blade (or an old plastic card) in one smooth stroke, at 45 degrees. Lift the stencil straight up. Under a magnifier, each pad has a clean block of paste, and the exposed pad shows a grid of rectangles, not one mass.

3. Place the RP2040. With fine tweezers, turn the chip so its pin 1 dot is at the pin 1 mark on the board. Lower it onto the paste without pressing. The paste is sticky enough to hold it. Check the position under the magnifier.

4. Place the flash chip. The W25Q128JV in SOIC-8 has legs and is easier. Put pin 1 (marked with a dot) at its mark.

5. Place the other surface-mount parts, smallest first: 0402 resistors and capacitors, then the SOT-223 LDO, the crystal, the WS2812B LED, the tactile switches, and the polyfuse.

6. **Reflow.** Put the board in a reflow oven, or hold a hot air station above it. Warm it to 150 C over 60 to 90 seconds, then raise it to a peak of 245 to 260 C for 30 to 60 seconds. The paste melts and becomes shiny. Remove the heat and let the board cool in still air: a sudden change of temperature can crack parts.

7. **Solder the through-hole headers.** After reflow, solder the two GPIO headers and the SWD header with an ordinary soldering iron.

8. **Inspect the QFN joints.** Look at the chip's edges from the side. A small, shiny slope of solder joins each pad to the package edge. Solder that joins two neighbouring pads is a bridge: remove it with flux and solder wick. Check with a meter that the chip's ground is joined to the board's ground.

9. **Check before first power.** A wiring fault on a USB board can damage the board and the port that feeds it. With a meter, check that no two of VBUS, 3.3V, 1.1V and GND are joined. Power the new board from a USB charger or a hub first, not from a computer, and measure 3.3V and 1.1V. If a part becomes hot, unplug at once.

</Steps>

## Firmware: USB Blink with UF2

<InArticleAd />


### Entering UF2 Bootloader Mode

The boot ROM of the RP2040 accepts a program as a `.uf2` file. No software is needed on the computer:

<Steps>

1. **Hold the BOOTSEL button.**

2. **Connect the USB-C cable to a computer** (or press reset while holding BOOTSEL if already connected).

3. **Release BOOTSEL.** The board appears as a USB mass storage device named "RPI-RP2."

4. **Drag and drop a `.uf2` file** onto the drive. The RP2040 flashes the firmware and reboots automatically.

</Steps>

### CircuitPython or MicroPython

The fastest test of the board is CircuitPython or MicroPython, two versions of Python that run on the chip. Each program below shows red, green and blue in turn on the LED:

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="CircuitPython">

Download the CircuitPython UF2 for "Raspberry Pi Pico" from [circuitpython.org](https://circuitpython.org/board/raspberry_pi_pico/). Drag the `.uf2` file onto the RPI-RP2 drive. The board reboots and appears as a "CIRCUITPY" drive.

Copy `neopixel.mpy` from the Adafruit CircuitPython library bundle into the `lib` folder on the CIRCUITPY drive, then create a file named `code.py` on the drive:

```python
import board
import neopixel
import time

# WS2812B on GPIO16 (adjust to your pin)
pixel = neopixel.NeoPixel(board.GP16, 1, brightness=0.3)

while True:
    pixel.fill((255, 0, 0))    # Red
    time.sleep(0.5)
    pixel.fill((0, 255, 0))    # Green
    time.sleep(0.5)
    pixel.fill((0, 0, 255))    # Blue
    time.sleep(0.5)
    pixel.fill((0, 0, 0))      # Off
    time.sleep(0.5)
```

### PIO Example: WS2812B LED Control

The WS2812B reads its colour from one wire at 800,000 bits per second. A 1 is a long high pulse and a 0 is a short one, and the difference is a fraction of a microsecond. A program that switches a pin in a loop cannot hold that timing when an interrupt arrives. A PIO state machine can, because it does nothing else.

This is the PIO program (from `ws2812.pio` in the pico-examples repository). Each bit takes T1 + T2 + T3 = 10 clock steps of the state machine:

```
; WS2812B PIO program
; Drives a WS2812B LED strip with 800kHz signal
.program ws2812
.side_set 1

.define public T1 2
.define public T2 5
.define public T3 3

.wrap_target
bitloop:
    out x, 1       side 0 [T3 - 1]  ; shift 1 bit, drive low
    jmp !x do_zero side 1 [T1 - 1]  ; if bit is 0, jump; drive high
do_one:
    jmp bitloop    side 1 [T2 - 1]  ; bit is 1: stay high longer
do_zero:
    nop            side 0 [T2 - 1]  ; bit is 0: drive low longer
.wrap
```

The state machine sends the colour data one bit at a time. The processor only writes each colour value into the state machine's queue (its FIFO) and continues with other work, so the timing stays exact whatever the two cores are doing.

The PIO assembler (`pioasm`, built with the Pico SDK) turns a `.pio` file into a C header that you include in your program.
